# A Decade of Rowhammer — From DRAM Disturbance to Production Mitigation

## Reading Guide

A reader new to the Rowhammer literature may approach this writeup along several paths. The chronological path runs §§2 through 7 in order, treating each as a successive falsification of the assumptions of its predecessors. The architectural-locus path begins with §1 (the physical phenomenon) and §1.5 (the access-pattern taxonomy), then samples §§2 through 7 in the order most relevant to the reader's substrate of interest. The mitigation path skips ahead to §8, returns to the attack sections for context, and ends with §10 (open questions). The shortest reading is §1, §8.5 (the obscurity pattern), and §9 (citation table); these together convey the field's structure and provide the bibliographic anchors for further reading.

The piece does not attempt to be a tutorial. It assumes that the reader has read at least one prior writeup in this collection (most usefully, Silicon Is the Adversary for a horizontal map of the microarchitectural surface, or Mathematical Collapse for the theoretical framing of trust-boundary failure). Terms specific to the Rowhammer literature are consolidated in Appendix on Terminology.

## Author's Note

Rowhammer is not a bug. It is the silicon doing what silicon does at scale.

A DRAM cell is a single capacitor whose stored charge represents one bit. The capacitor is small, the wordlines that activate it are close to its neighbors, and the dielectric that isolates one bitline from the next is thinner with every process node. When a row of cells is activated rapidly, the resulting electromagnetic and electrostatic transients on adjacent wordlines accelerate charge loss in cells that were never explicitly addressed. The phenomenon was first reported as a hardware-security concern in 2014. It has remained one in every generation since.

The pages that follow do not take a side. They do not coach an attacker on which pattern to load, and they do not coach a defender on which mitigation to procure. They map the literature the way a comparative-anatomy text maps related species: aggressor row, victim row, sampler, inhibitor, counter, alert pin. The objects exist and behave in ways that have been measured; the writeup records the measurements and the conditions under which they were taken.

There is a reason this anatomical register is the right one for Rowhammer in particular. The phenomenon sits at the intersection of three disciplines that rarely share a vocabulary: solid-state physics, computer architecture, and operating-system security. Attack-oriented writing tends to absorb the language of one of those disciplines and discard the others. Defense-oriented writing tends to flatten the physics into a checklist of mitigations whose internal mechanics are treated as black boxes. Neither posture preserves the artifact that the literature has actually produced, which is a decade-long natural history of how a single physical fault mode adapts to every counter-design that vendors have shipped.

Each section of this document closes with a short paragraph that names the assumption invalidated by the work covered in that section. The point of that closing paragraph is not rhetorical. The point is that the entire arc of Rowhammer research has been a sequence of falsifications: of the distance-1 hypothesis, of the uniformity hypothesis, of the access-count-only hypothesis, of the security-through-obscurity hypothesis, and of the DDR5-is-immune hypothesis. Reading the literature as a chain of negative results is more honest than reading it as a chain of new attacks.

This piece pairs with the SPI-Firmware-Anatomy writeup in this collection. Both are physical-hardware fault-surface readings; both deliberately resist the attacker/defender register; both treat the silicon and the standards as primary sources rather than as adversary or protection. The Silicon-Adversary writeup maps the microarchitectural surface horizontally; Microarch-Isolation reads it vertically along trust boundaries; this writeup walks one phenomenon — read disturbance in commodity DRAM — along the temporal axis from 2014 to 2026.

## Abstract

This writeup synthesises the academic literature on Rowhammer over the period 2014 to 2026, beginning with the original demonstration on DDR3 by Kim and colleagues at ISCA 2014 and ending with the Phoenix and QPRAC work on DDR5 with Per Row Activation Counting. The writeup proceeds anatomically rather than chronologically-only: each section identifies a physical or architectural locus, surveys the experimental work that characterised it, records the data points that subsequent papers have cited, and closes by naming the prior assumption that the work invalidated. The mitigation arc — Target Row Refresh, ECC, PARA, Graphene, BlockHammer, ProTRR, Refresh Management commands, Per Row Activation Counting, and the QPRAC priority queue — is read as a sequence of design responses to falsified assumptions rather than as a hierarchy of strength. The piece concludes with a research-agenda section and a citation table that consolidates roughly twenty foundational and follow-up papers.

## Note on Scope

The writeup addresses the academic and standards literature on Rowhammer through 2026. Several adjacent topics are not within scope and are mentioned only in passing.

Software-side mitigations — anti-Rowhammer hardware-performance-counter monitoring (ANVIL, ASPLOS 2016), kernel-side memory allocator hardening, OS-level isolation domains (Siloz, SOSP 2023) — are mentioned where they intersect the hardware mitigation story but are not surveyed in depth. The hardware-side mitigation is the primary subject; the software-side mitigations are responses to the hardware-side mitigation's incompleteness.

End-to-end exploit construction beyond the bit-flip-triggering layer — Page Table Entry manipulation, kernel privilege escalation chains, cross-VM attack engineering — is touched on in §§3, 4, and 5 where the source papers contain end-to-end exploit results but is not surveyed in depth. The exploit framework is older than the bit-flip-triggering framework and is well-documented in the security literature outside the Rowhammer corpus.

Browser-based Rowhammer — Rowhammer.js, SMASH, Posthammer — is referenced where it intersects the synchronisation and pattern axes but is not surveyed in depth. The browser context introduces timing-resolution and isolation constraints that are specific to the JavaScript execution model; treating them in detail would expand the scope considerably.

Specific vendor product details — particular DIMM model numbers, SPD configurations of specific batches, BIOS-update version histories — are not within scope. The literature is organised around vendor anonymisation and date ranges rather than around product-level specifics; the public record does not in general contain product-level data that would support more specific identification.

The non-academic Rowhammer literature — security-vendor reports, bug bounty disclosures, conference talks without published papers — is occasionally mentioned but not surveyed in depth. The academic record is the primary source for this writeup; the broader practitioner record extends but does not contradict the academic findings.

## Note on Naming and Conventions

The Rowhammer literature uses several naming conventions inconsistently. The phenomenon itself is called "Rowhammer" by most papers, "row hammer" (two words) by some IEEE conferences, "RowHammer" (capitalised) by the SAFARI group, and "Row Hammer" (Title Case) by some industry documents. This writeup uses "Rowhammer" throughout, with occasional capital-R variants where they appear in quotation. The activation-count threshold is called HCmin in TRRespass and Blacksmith and TRH in the JEDEC PRAC specification; the two terms refer to the same quantity. The mitigation threshold is called NBO in the PRAC specification and the mitigation threshold M in some academic proposals; this writeup uses NBO when discussing PRAC and the mitigation threshold M when discussing pre-PRAC academic proposals. The pattern variable for row-open time is tAggON in the RowPress paper, derived from the JEDEC tRAS notation; this writeup uses tAggON when discussing RowPress-class attacks.

The vendor anonymisation in academic papers is also inconsistent. TRRespass anonymises vendors as A, B, C, D, with A typically Samsung, B typically SK Hynix, C typically Micron. Blacksmith uses the same convention for DDR4 and a separate A/B/C convention for LPDDR4X. Half-Double anonymises commercial-SoC vendor information entirely. RowPress uses Mfr. S, H, M for Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron explicitly. Phoenix uses H0 through H14 for SK Hynix-only DIMM identifiers. This writeup uses the public vendor names where the source papers do, and uses the anonymised identifiers where the source papers do.

## §1 The Physical Phenomenon

A DRAM array is organised as a matrix of single-transistor, single-capacitor cells. The capacitor stores between roughly 10 and 30 femtocoulombs of charge in modern processes; the sense amplifier reads this charge by sharing it onto a precharged bitline and detecting whether the resulting voltage is above or below the precharge reference. The transistor that gates the capacitor is opened by raising the wordline of the row to which the cell belongs. Reading a cell therefore involves driving the wordline of one row of the array high, allowing every cell on that row to deposit its charge onto its bitline, latching the resulting bitline voltages in the sense amplifiers, and then optionally writing the latched values back to restore the original charges.

The geometric structure of a DRAM die is hierarchical. A die is divided into banks, banks into subarrays, subarrays into rows. A row in a DDR4 die spans on the order of 8 kilobits, divided into columns of 64 bits each. The wordline that activates a row runs across the array, controlling the gate of every cell on the row simultaneously. The bitlines run perpendicular to the wordlines, connecting cells in the same column down the length of the subarray. Adjacent rows in physical layout share both a sense-amplifier reference plane and the dielectric isolation of their cell capacitors. Adjacent rows in logical (row-address) layout are not necessarily adjacent in physical layout: the row-address-to-physical-row mapping is determined by the DRAM die's internal layout, and a row-address remapping table inside the die may further perturb the mapping for redundancy purposes. The Rowhammer literature treats logical adjacency as a proxy for physical adjacency, on the basis that the two are correlated in commodity parts; experimental work on the address mapping (DRAMA, Pessl et al., USENIX Security 2016) has demonstrated that the proxy is reliable within a single bank but breaks down across banks.

This sequence is encapsulated by the DRAM commands ACT (activate, raise the wordline), RD/WR (read or write columns from the row buffer that the sense amplifiers form), and PRE (precharge, lower the wordline and return bitlines to the reference voltage). The minimum activation interval, tRC, is on the order of 45 to 50 nanoseconds for DDR4 and slightly less for DDR5. A standard 64-millisecond refresh window for DDR4 (32 ms for DDR5) sets an upper bound of roughly 1.4 million activations per bank within one window if each ACT is followed immediately by a PRE.

The timing parameters that bound DRAM access are exposed in the JEDEC specification under standardised names. tRAS is the minimum interval that a row must remain open between an ACT and the next PRE; for DDR4 it is approximately 32 to 35 nanoseconds, manufacturer-dependent. tRP is the precharge time, the interval from a PRE to the next ACT, approximately 13 nanoseconds for DDR4-3200. tRCD is the activate-to-read delay, the interval from an ACT to the first valid RD or WR on the row, also approximately 13 nanoseconds. tRFC is the refresh cycle time, the interval that a REFRESH command occupies a bank; it is approximately 350 nanoseconds for DDR4 8 Gb parts. tREFI is the average refresh interval, the time between successive REFRESH commands, fixed at approximately 7.8 microseconds for DDR4 and 3.9 microseconds for DDR5. tREFW is the refresh window, the time within which every row must be refreshed at least once; it is 64 milliseconds for DDR4 and 32 milliseconds for DDR5. The product of tREFI and tREFW divided by the number of rows in a bank gives the number of REFRESH commands required to cover the bank within a window; in DDR4 the typical number is 8192 REFRESH commands per refresh window.

The refresh mechanism itself bears mention because every Rowhammer mitigation since 2014 has been a modification of it. A REFRESH command, when issued by the memory controller, refreshes a fixed set of rows within the addressed bank in a single tRFC interval. The set of rows refreshed by each REFRESH is determined internally by the DRAM, by reference to a refresh counter that increments on each REFRESH command. The memory controller does not know, and does not need to know, which specific rows are refreshed by any given REFRESH command. This opacity is what makes in-DRAM TRR feasible: the DRAM may, within the tRFC window of a REFRESH command, refresh additional rows beyond the ones the refresh counter prescribes, without the memory controller observing the difference.

The original physics of Rowhammer concerns what happens to the rows adjacent to a row that is activated many times in succession. The wordline of an activated row is at an elevated voltage for the duration that the row remains open (a duration parameterised by tAggON in the read-disturbance literature). Each wordline transition couples capacitively and inductively to the wordlines and bitlines of physically neighbouring rows. Repeated activation of a single row drives small currents through this coupling network. The effect on a neighbouring cell is a slow drift in the stored charge — too small to register from a single activation, but cumulative over many. Beyond a threshold number of activations, called the Rowhammer Threshold or HCmin, the drift exceeds the noise margin of the sense amplifier and a bit flips. Kim and colleagues measured this threshold at roughly 139,000 activations on the DDR3 modules of 2014; subsequent device studies have measured it at 45,000 on DDR4 and approximately 32,000 on DDR5 (Gloor et al., REFault, µASC 2025).

The downward trend in HCmin between generations reflects the technology scaling of the DRAM process. DDR3 was manufactured at process nodes in the range of 30 to 40 nanometers; DDR4 at 20 to 25 nanometers; DDR5 at 14 to 18 nanometers, depending on vendor and generation. Smaller cells have smaller capacitors and store less charge, which reduces the noise margin and the disturbance budget that the cell can absorb before a flip becomes detectable. Smaller cell pitch reduces the physical distance between adjacent rows, which strengthens the capacitive coupling per unit of wordline voltage transition. The two effects compound. The empirical scaling from 139,000 to 45,000 to 32,000 activations across three generations is consistent with both. Whether the trend continues for DDR6 and beyond is the subject of ongoing device-physics characterisation; the public projection, on the basis of node-scaling rates, places projected DDR6 HCmin below 20,000 activations.

Three secondary effects compound the primary one. First, the cell-level vulnerability is not uniform across a die: certain cells, often called weak cells, exhibit thresholds well below the population mean. Memory templating exploits this: by mapping the cell-level vulnerability of a region of memory, one identifies the cells that flip first. Second, the failure direction is data-dependent — a cell in the charged state (1 in true-cell convention, 0 in anti-cell convention) and a cell in the discharged state behave asymmetrically under stress. The dominant flip direction in classical Rowhammer is from the discharged to the charged state in true-cell layouts (0 to 1). Third, temperature accelerates the underlying charge-leakage processes. At 80°C the threshold is consistently lower than at 50°C across all major vendors.

The data pattern stored in the aggressor and victim rows also influences the bit-flip rate. The checkerboard pattern, in which the aggressor row holds 0xAA and the victim row holds 0x55 (or vice versa), has been established as the most disturbing pattern for classical Rowhammer on DDR4 by Tatar et al. (RAID 2018) and confirmed across subsequent studies. The reason is that the checkerboard maximises the bitline voltage transitions during sense-amplifier resolution, which maximises the disturbance budget injected into the adjacent cell. Other patterns — solid 0x00, solid 0xFF, walking 1s, walking 0s — produce fewer flips on average. The pattern dependence is the reason that templating campaigns initialise aggressor and victim rows with specific values before each scan: the goal is to maximise the probability of detecting any flip the cell is capable of.

The geometric structure of the failure population matters for exploitation. A single bit flip in a region of memory that holds executable code, page tables, or cryptographic keys can be exploitable; a flip in a region holding scratch data may not be. The exploitation literature has consolidated on three classical target classes — Page Table Entries, RSA-2048 modulus bits, and sudo-binary opcode bits — for which the exploitability conditions are well characterised. The PTE attack flips a bit in a page table entry's Physical Frame Number field, redirecting the virtual page to a physical frame that is itself a page table; an attacker who maps the redirected page in their own address space gains a read/write primitive over the kernel page-table hierarchy. The RSA-2048 attack flips a bit in the modulus of a long-lived public key, producing a composite modulus that is factorable with non-negligible probability; the factorisation completes the key compromise. The sudo attack flips a specific opcode bit in the in-memory copy of the sudo binary, converting a conditional branch into an unconditional one and bypassing the authentication check. The three attacks together establish that the exploitability of a Rowhammer primitive is not a single number but a function of which target class is in scope: PTE attacks require the broadest target population and are the easiest, sudo attacks require the narrowest and are the hardest.

The mechanism is intrinsically physical. No software layer above the memory controller has visibility into wordline transitions; the operating system sees only loads and stores at addresses that the memory controller routes to banks, rows, and columns through a vendor-specific addressing function. The vulnerability lives below every abstraction that the software stack exposes.

The addressing function itself merits brief description because it is the bridge between the software-visible address and the physically meaningful row identifier. Memory controllers compute the bank, row, and column for a given physical address by applying a deterministic mapping function. The function is typically implemented as a sequence of XOR operations over selected bits of the physical address; the bit selection and XOR structure are chosen by the memory-controller designer to distribute consecutive cache lines across banks for parallelism. The DRAMA paper (Pessl et al., USENIX Security 2016) reverse-engineered these functions for several Intel platforms by observing the row-conflict latency between accesses to pairs of physical addresses: two addresses in the same row exhibit the lowest latency, two addresses in different rows of the same bank exhibit the highest, and two addresses in different banks exhibit an intermediate latency. By measuring the latencies for many address pairs and clustering by latency class, the DRAMA methodology recovers the addressing function up to an unknown permutation of the bank identifiers.

The recovery is sensitive to platform details that the public documentation does not always disclose. The ZenHammer paper (§7) found that the standard DRAMA methodology fails on AMD Zen because of an undocumented physical-address-space offset introduced by the AMD Memory Hoisting mechanism; the same methodology continues to work on Intel platforms unmodified. The implication is that the addressing function is not a property of the DRAM module alone but a property of the (memory controller × motherboard × BIOS configuration × DRAM module) tuple, with each component contributing constraints. An attack that requires knowledge of the addressing function must either reverse-engineer it for each tuple it targets or use a function-discovery technique (such as the Z3-based recovery in Half-Double) that operates without prior knowledge of the function structure.

**Assumption invalidated.** Up to 2014 it was a working assumption that DRAM cell isolation, as specified in JEDEC standards, was tight enough to guarantee that the charge of one cell was unaffected by activity in neighbouring cells within the refresh window. The data in §2 falsifies that assumption for every commodity device manufactured since the 30 nm node.

## §1.5 The Access-Pattern Taxonomy

Before examining the chronological development of the literature, it is useful to consolidate the access-pattern taxonomy that the literature has developed for describing Rowhammer attacks. The taxonomy has grown organically across a decade of papers; the consolidated version that follows is the post-2022 standard.

A Rowhammer access pattern is a sequence of memory operations executed within a refresh window. Each operation activates a specific DRAM row and then either reads or writes within that row before another operation activates a different row. The activation rate of any particular row is the number of times that row is activated within the refresh window. The aggressor rows are the rows whose activation produces the disturbance that flips a target victim row; the victim row is the row that the attacker hopes to flip; the decoy rows are rows whose activation is included to satisfy timing constraints (preventing the row buffer from retaining state) or to confuse the in-DRAM mitigation (filling the sampler with addresses that the attacker does not care about).

The pattern's spatial structure describes which rows are activated and at what distances from the victim. Single-sided patterns activate one aggressor row at distance 1 from the victim, plus decoys. Double-sided patterns activate two aggressor rows at distance 1 from the victim, one on each side. Many-sided patterns activate three or more aggressor rows, with the precise spatial arrangement varying across the variants. Distance-2 patterns (Half-Double) activate aggressors at distance 2 from the victim, with the distance-1 activations contributed by the TRR inhibitor.

The pattern's temporal structure describes how the activations are distributed in time. Uniform patterns activate each aggressor with equal frequency, distributing the total activation budget evenly across the aggressor set. Non-uniform patterns (Blacksmith) activate aggressors with distinct frequencies, phases, and amplitudes, producing a temporal distribution that the in-DRAM mitigation cannot easily summarise. Frequency-domain patterns parameterise the temporal distribution in the (frequency, phase, amplitude) tuple space, treating the access pattern as a signal whose properties can be controlled in the frequency domain.

The pattern's duration structure describes how long each activated row is held open. Classical patterns hold each row at tRAS minimum, approximately 36 nanoseconds. RowPress patterns hold each row open for substantially longer durations, parameterised by tAggON. Combined patterns vary both the temporal distribution and the duration, exploring a two-dimensional search space.

The pattern's synchronisation structure describes how the pattern aligns with the underlying REFRESH commands issued by the memory controller. Synchronised patterns detect REFRESH events (via timing side channels) and align pattern boundaries with REFRESH boundaries. Unsynchronised patterns run free, accepting that some pattern executions will misalign and produce no flips. Self-correcting patterns (Phoenix) detect when REFRESH events have been missed and realign the pattern execution to recover.

The combination of these axes — spatial, temporal, duration, synchronisation — defines a high-dimensional pattern search space. Different attack papers explore different regions of the space: TRRespass varies the cardinality of the spatial axis; Blacksmith varies the temporal axis; Half-Double varies the distance axis; RowPress varies the duration axis; ZenHammer varies the synchronisation axis; Phoenix combines temporal, synchronisation, and DDR5-specific period axes. The cumulative result is that the search space is multidimensional, that the dimensions can be searched independently, and that the most effective attacks combine multiple axes simultaneously.

The taxonomy generalises naturally to mitigation analysis. A mitigation is characterised by which axes it monitors and how it responds to anomalous patterns on those axes. In-DRAM TRR monitors the spatial-cardinality axis (the number of distinct rows recently activated) and the temporal-frequency axis (the rate at which each row is activated). PRAC monitors the spatial-per-row axis (the activation count of each individual row) and the temporal-cumulative axis (the cumulative activation budget within the refresh window). RowPress-aware mitigations (when deployed) would add the duration axis. Half-Double-aware mitigations would address the distance-2 composition. Each mitigation's coverage is determined by which axes it monitors; an axis that no mitigation monitors is exposed to attacks that operate primarily along that axis.

## §2 The First Demonstration (2014)

Kim, Daly, Kim, Fallin, Lee, Lee, Wilkerson, Lai, and Mutlu published "Flipping Bits in Memory Without Accessing Them: An Experimental Study of DRAM Disturbance Errors" at the 41st International Symposium on Computer Architecture (ISCA 2014). The paper is the empirical founding document of the field. Its experimental scope was 129 DDR3 modules from three major vendors, manufactured between 2008 and 2014. The methodology was straightforward in retrospect: identify pairs of adjacent rows, activate each pair repeatedly with a tight loop of `mov` and `clflush` instructions executed from user space on a contemporary Intel platform, and scan the surrounding rows for bit flips between activation campaigns.

The headline numbers were unambiguous. Approximately 85% of the tested DDR3 modules exhibited at least one bit flip within the experimental budget. The minimum activation count required to trigger a flip — HCmin in the paper's notation — ranged from 139,000 to roughly 1,000,000 across modules. The flips were not uniformly distributed across the cells of a vulnerable module; instead they concentrated on weak cells whose retention characteristics were already at the tail of the population distribution. The flips appeared in rows adjacent to the row that had been activated, supporting an intuitive electromagnetic-coupling model.

Three structural observations from the paper deserve isolation, because they framed the next decade of work.

The first is that the phenomenon is universal across vendors. The paper tested modules from Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron in roughly equal proportions, and recorded vulnerable cells in modules from all three. The phenomenon was not a quirk of one process line. It was an emergent property of the technology node.

The second is that the effect is intrinsic to the DRAM substrate rather than to any property of the host CPU or the memory controller. The paper used the same Intel test platform across all 129 modules; the variation in HCmin reflected the modules, not the host. Vendor-side discussion of "host-side fixes" — increased refresh rates, BIOS countermeasures — was therefore from the outset a workaround, not a fix.

The third is that the activation patterns required were modest. The paper described two pattern classes that the subsequent literature inherited: single-sided, where one aggressor row is hammered amid a set of decoy rows that prevent the row buffer from retaining state, and double-sided, where two aggressor rows on either side of a target victim are hammered in alternation. Double-sided patterns produced more bit flips per activation budget than single-sided ones on every module that exhibited flips at all.

The industry response in 2014 and 2015 was twofold. Intel introduced pseudo-TRR (pTRR) in the Ivy Bridge EP server-class memory controllers: the memory controller read a Maximum Activate Count (MAC) field from the module's Serial Presence Detect (SPD) ROM, tracked activation counts in the memory controller, and issued a row refresh when a configured threshold was crossed. BIOS vendors shipped firmware updates that doubled the standard refresh rate, halving the time between refresh commands from 7.8 µs to 3.9 µs. Neither response was a substrate fix. They added cost, in performance and in silicon area, against a measurable but not characterised threat.

The JEDEC MAC field encoding deserves brief description because it became the lingua franca of vendor Rowhammer claims through the late 2010s. The MAC field is one byte of SPD ROM data; values 0b0001 through 0b0110 specify discrete activation thresholds from 200,000 to 700,000; value 0b1000 specifies "unlimited" (the module claims Rowhammer immunity at any activation count); value 0b0000 specifies "untested." The pTRR mechanism in supported Intel memory controllers reads this field at boot, configures its internal activation threshold accordingly, and issues mitigative refreshes when a row's count exceeds the threshold. Vendors who shipped DDR4 modules with in-DRAM TRR set the MAC field to "unlimited," signalling that no host-side mitigation was required. The TRRespass paper later showed by direct measurement that the unlimited claim was a marketing artifact rather than a verifiable property: every modern module with the unlimited MAC field, on bypass of the in-DRAM TRR, exhibited bit flips at activation counts well below any practical interpretation of "unlimited."

By 2016 the DDR3 deployment base was beginning to be replaced by DDR4, and DRAM vendors began shipping DDR4 modules with the SPD MAC field set to "unlimited" — the JEDEC encoding that claims the module is, by the vendor's testing, immune to Rowhammer at any activation count within the refresh window. The claim rested on an undocumented in-DRAM mitigation that vendors did not specify in public datasheets. That mitigation came to be called Target Row Refresh.

Between 2014 and 2019 the public Rowhammer attack literature continued to develop primitives on the DDR3 base, even as DDR3 was being phased out. Drammer (Van der Veen et al., CCS 2016) demonstrated deterministic Rowhammer on mobile platforms, with end-to-end privilege escalation on Android devices that used LPDDR3 and early LPDDR4. Rowhammer.js (Gruss et al., DIMVA 2016) demonstrated Rowhammer from JavaScript executing in a browser, removing the requirement for native code execution on the target system. Flip Feng Shui (Razavi et al., USENIX Security 2016) demonstrated cross-VM Rowhammer in a cloud co-tenancy setting, against an RSA-2048 SSH key held in the page cache of a victim VM that was deduplicated against an attacker-controlled VM's memory. The sudo binary attack (Gruss et al., S&P 2018) demonstrated single-bit-flip exploitation of a setuid binary's authentication check. ECC.fail (Cojocar et al., S&P 2019) demonstrated that server-class memory with ECC was not Rowhammer-immune in practice, because the ECC mechanism could be defeated by accumulating multiple flips within an ECC codeword. Each of these papers extended the exploitation surface; none of them, by 2019, had produced a single bit flip on a DDR4 module with active in-DRAM TRR.

### The DRAM array hierarchy

DRAM organisation has several layers of physical hierarchy that the Rowhammer literature references with specific terminology. A DRAM channel is a single command-and-data interface between the memory controller and one or more DIMM modules; commodity systems typically have two or four channels in parallel. A DIMM (Dual In-line Memory Module) carries one or more ranks, where a rank is a set of DRAM chips that respond to a single chip-select signal and operate in lockstep. A rank consists of typically 8 to 18 chips, with each chip contributing 4 or 8 bits to the rank's 64-bit (non-ECC) or 72-bit (ECC) data bus. A chip is divided into banks (16 banks per chip on DDR4, 32 banks per chip on DDR5); a bank is divided into subarrays; a subarray is divided into rows. The Rowhammer effect occurs within a subarray: aggressor and victim must be in the same subarray of the same bank of the same chip. Inter-subarray and inter-bank effects, where they exist, are of significantly lower magnitude and have not been demonstrated as primary attack vectors in the literature.

The bit-flip distribution across the chips of a rank is one of the key dimensions of cross-chip variability that the characterisation papers track. The Blacksmith paper's chip-dependence finding — that 65% of the 40 surveyed DIMMs exhibited byte-offset asymmetries in their bit-flip distributions — reflects the fact that the 8 chips in a rank operate in lockstep but are physically distinct dice with independent process variation. A pattern that is effective on the most-vulnerable chip of a rank may produce no flips on the least-vulnerable chip; for an attacker whose exploitation target is at a specific byte offset within the data bus, the relevant population is the chip that handles that byte offset, not the rank as a whole. The implication is that exploitation success depends on the joint distribution of bit-flip vulnerability across chips and exploitation-target placement within the data bus, neither of which the attacker controls directly.

### The 2014 access loop

The Kim et al. measurement methodology used a tight loop in user-space C code that consisted of three instructions per aggressor row activation: a load from a virtual address that mapped to the aggressor row, a `clflush` to evict the corresponding cache line from the cache hierarchy, and an implicit memory barrier (subsequent papers added explicit `mfence` instructions for ordering). The loop iterated approximately 540,000 times per refresh window on the Intel test platform, alternating between two aggressor rows (the double-sided pattern) on either side of a target victim row. The total wall-clock time required to reach the activation budget was approximately 64 milliseconds — one refresh window — after which the scan phase began.

The scan phase, also described in the 2014 paper, walked every row in the experimental region of memory and recorded any cell whose value differed from its initialised data pattern. The scan ran in a separate phase from the hammer loop to avoid perturbing the access pattern; the scan itself involved row activations that would, in principle, contribute to the disturbance budget if it overlapped the hammer phase. Subsequent papers refined this by alternating short hammer bursts with short scan bursts, but the 2014 methodology established the basic structure: hammer to budget, scan to detect, record the cells that flipped.

The 2014 paper also reported one of the earliest measurements of the data-pattern dependence of bit flips. The paper tested four data-pattern combinations: aggressor 0x00 with victim 0xFF; aggressor 0xFF with victim 0x00; aggressor 0xAA with victim 0x55; aggressor 0x55 with victim 0xAA. The checkerboard combinations (0xAA/0x55 and 0x55/0xAA) produced more bit flips than the solid combinations (0x00/0xFF and 0xFF/0x00) by a factor of approximately two on the most vulnerable modules. The result has been re-confirmed by every subsequent characterisation study; the checkerboard combination became the standard data pattern for templating campaigns.

**Assumption invalidated.** The pre-2014 working assumption was that DRAM read disturbance, if it existed, was a manufacturing defect that quality control would catch before shipment. The Kim et al. data falsifies that assumption: the disturbance is not a defect but a substrate-level emergent property of the technology node, present in roughly 85% of randomly selected modules.

## §3 The TRR Era and the TRRespass Response (2020)

Between 2014 and 2019 the public Rowhammer literature continued to produce attack papers — Drammer for mobile devices (Van der Veen et al., CCS 2016), Rowhammer.js for browser execution (Gruss et al., DIMVA 2016), Flip Feng Shui for cloud co-tenancy (Razavi et al., USENIX Security 2016), the sudo binary-page attack (Gruss et al., S&P 2018), the ECC.fail study (Cojocar et al., S&P 2019) — but none of these papers triggered bit flips on the new DDR4 generation. Vendor marketing aligned with this empirical silence: DDR4 with the in-DRAM mitigation was sold as Rowhammer-free.

In March 2020, Frigo, Vannacci, Hassan, Van der Veen, Mutlu, Giuffrida, Bos, and Razavi published "TRRespass: Exploiting the Many Sides of Target Row Refresh" at IEEE Security and Privacy 2020. The paper received the conference's Best Paper Award and a Pwnie nomination for Most Innovative Research. It is the empirical refutation of the "DDR4 is immune" claim.

The paper's experimental scope was 42 DDR4 modules from Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron, manufactured between 2016 and 2019. The paper's first finding was a null result that established the baseline: classical single-sided, double-sided, and one-location patterns triggered zero bit flips on any of the 42 modules. The null result confirmed the vendor claim under classical attack methodologies; the rest of the paper undid it.

The paper's structural contribution had three parts.

The first part was the demystification of TRR as a term. Until TRRespass, "TRR" was a single label that covered at least three distinct mechanisms: Intel pTRR in server memory controllers, BIOS-level double refresh rate, and the undocumented in-DRAM TRR that vendors had been shipping in DDR4 modules since approximately 2016. The paper showed by experiment that pTRR was deployed only on a single tested Intel server platform (Xeon E5-2620 v2) and absent from all tested consumer CPUs. Consumer DDR4 systems relied entirely on the in-DRAM mechanism, which was vendor-specific, undocumented, and accessible only via reverse engineering.

The second part was the reverse engineering itself. Using SoftMC (Hassan et al., HPCA 2017), an FPGA-based memory controller that allowed manual issuance of every DRAM command at cycle accuracy, the authors characterised the in-DRAM TRR of two case-study modules in detail. The model that emerged from the characterisation contained two components: a sampler, which tracked a small number of recently activated rows (4 to 6 in the studied modules), and an inhibitor, which issued a single targeted refresh per REFRESH command to one of the sampled rows' neighbours. The mitigation was therefore not a counter but a streaming top-k estimator with very limited memory. The sampler's tracking capacity was the critical resource. With a tracker holding only 4 to 6 rows, an attack that hammered more aggressors than the tracker could remember would overflow the tracker, leaving some aggressors unsampled and their victims unprotected.

The two case-study modules carried the bulk of the paper's reverse-engineering data. Module C12, a Micron 2015-production DDR4 module, was the more transparent of the two. Its in-DRAM TRR behaviour was characterised by what TRRespass called Observations 1 through 4: the mitigation acts on every REFRESH command (Observation 1, demonstrated by showing that even a single REFRESH dramatically reduces bit flips); the sampler samples more than one aggressor per REFRESH interval (Observation 2, demonstrated by showing that even three-sided patterns with a single REFRESH still produce some bit flips); the inhibitor refreshes only one victim per tRFC (Observation 3, demonstrated by the plateau in bit-flip reduction as the number of REFRESH commands per pattern increases); and the sampler size on C12 is approximately 4 rows (Observation 4, inferred from the plateau at r ≥ 4 aggressors). Module A15, a Samsung 2017-production DDR4 module, was less transparent. Its sampler size was approximately 6 rows, but the sampling behaviour was command-order-dependent (Observation 5) and row-address-dependent (Observation 6) in ways that the C12 sampler was not. The paper was candid about the difficulty of reproducing the SoftMC results on commodity hardware for A15: the precise command-order synchronisation that the FPGA-based controller provided was not achievable on commodity Intel memory controllers, and the bit-flip counts on commodity hardware were therefore lower than those measured on SoftMC.

The inferred model of the in-DRAM TRR mechanism — sampler plus inhibitor, with bounded sampler capacity — has continued to be useful as a framework for understanding subsequent attacks and mitigations. The sampler's capacity is the resource that TRRespass's many-sided overflow targets. The sampler's tracking algorithm is the resource that Blacksmith's non-uniform patterns target: a frequent-item estimator that has worst-case inputs at near-uniform distributions, which is what non-uniform patterns produce when the sampler attempts to identify the top-k frequent items in a population where many items have similar effective frequencies. The inhibitor's behaviour — issuing a single targeted refresh per REFRESH — is the resource that Half-Double targets: the refresh issued by the inhibitor activates a row that is itself the near aggressor in a distance-2 attack. Each of the three subsequent attacks corresponds to a different component of the inferred model, suggesting that the model captured the essential mechanism even though the implementation details varied across vendors.

The third part was the many-sided pattern class. By extending the double-sided template with additional aggressor rows — 3-sided, 5-sided, 9-sided, up to 19-sided in the case of two Samsung 2017 modules — the paper produced patterns that consistently overflowed the in-DRAM sampler. Of the 42 tested modules, 13 produced bit flips under at least one many-sided pattern. The most vulnerable module was a Micron 2015-production module (designated C12 in the paper) that produced 190,037 bit flips in a 256 MB sweep under a 10-sided pattern. The same module produced a Page Table Entry exploitable flip in 2.3 seconds of templating.

The TRRespass fuzzer that produced these patterns was, structurally, a simple program. Its loop had two random parameters: the cardinality `n` of the many-sided pattern (between 3 and 28 aggressors, bounded above by the activation budget per refresh window) and the location of the aggressor rows within a 128-row window in the test memory. For each iteration, the fuzzer chose values for the two parameters, generated a round-robin hammer pattern that visited each aggressor `H` times per refresh window (with `H × n` chosen to fill the activation budget), and scanned the surrounding rows for bit flips. Successful patterns were recorded; unsuccessful ones were discarded. The simplicity of the fuzzer is itself part of the paper's contribution: a two-parameter black-box search produced the bit flips that the entire prior literature had been unable to find on TRR-protected DDR4. The implication was that the search space of effective attacks against in-DRAM TRR was not large; the prior literature had simply not searched it.

The substrate finding that emerged from disabling REFRESH and measuring HCmin directly was, in hindsight, the most consequential of the paper's contributions. With TRR bypassed, the DDR4 substrate produced bit flips at approximately 45,000 activations — well below the 139,000 threshold that Kim et al. had measured on DDR3 six years earlier. The DDR4 substrate was more, not less, vulnerable than its predecessor. The apparent improvement in security between generations was an artefact of the mitigation, not an improvement in the underlying cells.

This finding inverts the standard narrative of generational hardware improvement. The DDR3-to-DDR4 transition was sold by vendors as a security improvement: faster, denser, and Rowhammer-resistant. The TRRespass measurement establishes that two of these three claims are correct (DDR4 is faster and denser than DDR3) and that the third is the reverse of what was claimed. DDR4 cells are weaker than DDR3 cells in their substrate resistance to disturbance; the apparent security improvement came entirely from the in-DRAM mitigation that hid the weakness. When the mitigation is bypassed, the substrate's true behaviour is exposed. The same inversion later applied to the DDR4-to-DDR5 transition: the REFault measurement of approximately 32,000 activations for DDR5 HCmin (Gloor et al., µASC 2025) is lower than the DDR4 measurement of 45,000, indicating that the DDR5 substrate is again weaker than its predecessor, with the apparent improvement coming from the strengthened mitigation. The trend has held across two generations and is consistent with the technology-scaling explanation: each generation's process node produces smaller, less robust cells, with the security narrative restored by progressively more complex in-DRAM mitigations.

The TRRespass fuzzer methodology and source were published. Within months, the methodology had been ported to JavaScript (SMASH, de Ridder et al., USENIX Security 2021), refined for non-uniform patterns (Blacksmith, Jattke et al., S&P 2022), and adapted to the AMD platform (ZenHammer, USENIX Security 2024). Every subsequent DDR4 and DDR5 attack paper in the academic literature inherits the SoftMC- or DRAM-Bender-based experimental infrastructure that TRRespass standardised.

The vendor distribution of TRRespass's 42-DIMM test set, in retrospect, conveyed substantive information about implementation heterogeneity. Samsung modules (Vendor A in the paper) produced bit flips in 7 of 16 tested DIMMs, with the most effective patterns ranging from 3-sided to 19-sided. SK Hynix modules (Vendor B) produced flips in 4 of 12 DIMMs, with all effective patterns being 3-sided and producing small bit-flip counts (5 to 22 flips per DIMM). Micron modules (Vendor C) produced flips in 2 of 14 DIMMs, with one outlier (the 2015-production C12 module) producing 190,037 flips under a 10-sided pattern. The vendor-by-vendor variation indicated that the in-DRAM TRR mechanism, far from being a single industry-wide design, was at least three distinct implementations with different sampler sizes, different inhibitor policies, and different sensitivities to pattern structure. The single TRRespass paper produced enough characterisation data to support hypotheses about each vendor's implementation, but the case studies (C12, A15) that the paper carried through in detail addressed only two of the three vendor classes.

The TRRespass exploitation results consolidated the practical significance of the bit-flip data. On the C12 module, the PTE attack produced an exploitable flip in 2.3 seconds of templating; the RSA-2048 attack in 74.6 seconds; the sudo attack in 54 minutes 16 seconds. On the A14 Samsung module, the PTE attack required 4.9 seconds, the RSA attack 6 minutes 27 seconds. The 13 modules that exhibited bit flips were not all exploitable for all three target classes — Hynix Vendor B modules with low absolute flip counts were not exploitable for any of the three — but the most vulnerable modules were exploitable for the full classical exploit suite. The 2014 to 2018 exploit templates, written against DDR3 substrate or against DDR4 in the brief window before TRR became universal, worked unchanged against modern TRR-protected DDR4. The Rowhammer exploit framework was decoupled from the bit-flip-triggering layer; updating the bit-flip-triggering layer (TRRespass) sufficed to re-enable the entire exploitation suite.

**Assumption invalidated.** The 2016 to 2020 working assumption was that the in-DRAM TRR mechanism, by virtue of being unspecified, was secure: vendors had argued that an attacker who could not see the mechanism could not bypass it. TRRespass falsifies that assumption directly. The discovery cost of the mechanism was on the order of two person-years; the replication cost, given a published fuzzer, was on the order of hours per module. Obscurity raised the discovery cost but did not raise the long-term cost of breaking the mechanism. The cost-asymmetry was fatal.

## §4 Many-Sided, Non-Uniform, Frequency-Domain

The TRRespass many-sided pattern class assumed uniform hammering: every aggressor in a pattern was activated at the same frequency, in regular alternation, for the duration of the experiment. The uniformity assumption was inherited tacitly from the 2014 to 2019 attack literature, which had no reason to vary it because double-sided uniform hammering was the optimal pattern under the absence of any mitigation. With TRR present, uniformity took on a structural meaning that the literature did not initially notice. A top-k frequent-item estimator, which is what the in-DRAM sampler effectively was, has worst-case inputs and easy inputs. Uniform hammering is the easy input: every aggressor in a many-sided pattern appears with the same frequency, and a tracker of capacity k > number-of-aggressors trivially identifies the top-k. Overflowing the tracker required more aggressors than the tracker had slots — TRRespass's approach — but it did not require deviating from uniformity within the slot count budget.

Two 2021 to 2022 papers broke the uniformity assumption from two different directions.

### Half-Double (Google / Graz, USENIX Security 2022)

In May 2021, Google's Chromium Security Team published a blog post announcing Half-Double: a Rowhammer effect at distance 2 from the activated row rather than at the adjacent distance 1. The technical paper, by Kogler, Juffinger, Qazi, Kim, Lipp, Boichat, Shiu, Nissler, and Gruss, appeared at USENIX Security 2022 after a fifteen-month embargo coordinated through the affected DRAM and SoC vendors. The disclosure pattern reflected the constraint structure of industry-academic Rowhammer research: the academic authors and the Google authors were bound by separate contractual obligations that limited what could be said about specific vendor implementations.

The Half-Double mechanism is anatomically distinct from many-sided overflow. Given three rows F, N, and V at distances 0, 1, and 2 from a victim, hammering F (the distance-2 row, called the far aggressor) many times produces bit flips in V (the distance-2 victim) only when accompanied by a handful of activations of N (the distance-1 row, called the near aggressor). The far aggressor alone produces no flips because its distance-2 electromagnetic coupling to V is too weak. The near aggressor alone produces no flips because the number of N activations the attacker can perform within a refresh window is insufficient to accumulate the required disturbance budget. The compositional effect arises because the activations of N that contribute the missing disturbance are not generated by the attacker. They are generated by TRR.

The notational framework introduced in §3 of the Half-Double paper has since become the standard for describing Rowhammer access patterns in the post-2022 literature. Victim rows are labelled V; near aggressors at distance 1 are labelled N+ and N− to indicate which side of the victim they lie on; far aggressors at distance 2 are labelled F+ and F−; decoys at greater distances are labelled D+ and D−. Pattern sequences are written with arrow-and-exponent notation: (N+ → N−)^∞ for classical double-sided; (F+ → F−)^∞ for the Half-Double Quad pattern; (N+_1 → N+_2 → ... → N+_k)^∞ for the TRRespass-style many-sided pattern with k same-side aggressors. The notation makes the distance structure of the pattern explicit and permits direct comparison of attacks that vary only in distance versus those that vary in temporal distribution.

The TRR inhibitor's response to repeated activation of F is to issue mitigative refreshes targeted at F's neighbours, of which N is one. Each mitigative refresh is, at the DRAM command level, an ACT followed by a PRE on N. The activation drains charge from the rows adjacent to N — including V. The cumulative effect of (attacker activations of F) plus (TRR-issued activations of N) is sufficient to flip bits in V. The mitigation is, in the literal command-sequence sense, performing part of the attack.

The paper's title formulation in the original text — "the cure becomes the disease" — describes this composition. The empirical validation of the mechanism was thorough: the authors disabled TRR via vendor cooperation on a commercial SoC platform and observed zero bit flips at distance 2; they re-enabled TRR and recovered the bit flips; they tested null-hypothesis patterns in which the far aggressors were replaced with decoys and observed a drop at the expected dilution level; they validated the result on a noise-free FPGA platform (the Antmicro LiteX Rowhammer Tester) with three DDR4 DIMMs, two of which exhibited the effect.

The paper also produced an end-to-end exploit on a Chromebook with TRR, ECC, and a current Chrome OS kernel, reaching root in an average of 45 minutes across ten runs with 100% success. The exploit assembled four independently nontrivial primitives: contiguous-memory detection without `/proc/pagemap` (achieved via a Z3 solver over the DRAM addressing functions), an ECC-aware blind-hammering technique that bypassed templating, child-process page-table spraying to compensate for ARM 3-level page tables, and a Spectre-based oracle for crash-safe verification of which PTE had flipped. The Spectre oracle is the more philosophically interesting of the four: it uses speculative-execution suppression on ARM Cortex-A cores — a property that is part of why those cores are not Meltdown-vulnerable — as a crash-safe read primitive over which-PTEs-are-still-valid after the hammer campaign. The defensive property became an offensive one.

The four challenges that the Half-Double end-to-end exploit overcame illustrate the depth of engineering work that a modern Rowhammer attack on a hardened target requires. The contiguous-memory challenge (C1 in the paper) was that the Quad pattern requires five physically adjacent rows, of which the middle one is the victim and is not mapped in the attacker's address space; without `/proc/pagemap`, the attacker cannot identify physically contiguous virtual ranges by inspection. The paper describes three approaches of increasing generality. The first uses Transparent Huge Pages (THP), which guarantee 2 MB-aligned physical regions and reveal the low 21 bits of the physical address. The second uses a row-conflict timing side channel (the DRAMA technique) combined with the buddy allocator's predictable behaviour to identify contiguous regions across THP boundaries. The third uses a Z3 SAT solver to recover the unknown DRAM addressing function from observed row-conflict patterns, on the assumption that the function is XOR-based; the solver returns either the recovered function and a contiguous region, or unsatisfiable (the region is not contiguous). The three approaches trade generality for speed: the THP approach runs in under 10 seconds, the page-distance approach in under 3 minutes, and the Z3 solver at approximately 1 MB per second.

The blind-hammering technique (C2) replaces classical templating, which is incompatible with on-die ECC. Classical templating fills aggressor rows with one pattern and victim rows with the complementary pattern, hammers, and scans for cells whose values have changed; the vulnerable cells are noted, and in the exploit phase the same hammering is repeated with the exploit-target data (a PTE, an RSA key) at the noted offsets. With ECC, single-bit flips are corrected silently and do not appear in the scan. The blind-hammering technique replaces the templating phase with a probabilistic approach: the attacker arranges memory so that target data (page tables) is at known offsets in the victim rows, hammers the entire region, and observes the side effects of corrupted page tables (a corrupted PTE typically leads the kernel to either return invalid data or terminate the offending process). The bit flip's existence is inferred from its consequence rather than from direct observation.

The child-spray technique (C3) addresses the ARM-specific constraint that 64-bit ARM systems often use a 3-level page table hierarchy, which limits user-space virtual address space to 512 GiB. With only 1 GiB of page tables addressable in a single process, the attacker cannot spray enough page tables to ensure that a hammer-induced bit flip will land on a target PTE with high probability. The fix is to fork multiple child processes, each of which contributes its own page-table hierarchy to the system without duplicating the underlying mapped data pages; the total page-table coverage scales with the number of children, allowing the attacker to occupy a larger fraction of physical memory with page tables and increase the probability that any given bit flip falls on a target PTE.

The Spectre oracle (C4) is the C2 component's solution to the verification problem. Once the blind-hammering campaign has produced bit flips, the attacker must identify which PTEs have been corrupted without dereferencing the corrupted PTEs (which would result in process termination). The oracle uses a Spectre-style speculative-execution gadget that loads from an address calculated from the bits of a candidate PTE; the speculative load fetches into the cache iff the PTE's bits constitute a valid kernel address. After the gadget runs, the attacker measures the cache state of the speculative target via Flush+Reload; a cache hit indicates that the speculative load succeeded, which indicates that the PTE was valid. The technique relies on the fact that ARM Cortex-A cores do not transient-leak faulting loads — they suppress the speculative path of a load that would fault, which means that an invalid PTE produces no cache state change. This property is precisely what makes ARM cores not Meltdown-vulnerable; the Half-Double oracle uses the absence of the Meltdown leak as an absence-of-evidence signal that confirms PTE invalidity.

### Blacksmith (ETH / VU, IEEE S&P 2022)

Six months after the Half-Double announcement, Jattke, Van der Veen, Frigo, Gunter, and Razavi published Blacksmith at IEEE S&P 2022. The paper's central observation was that the published Rowhammer literature, including TRRespass, had hammered aggressors uniformly: every aggressor in a many-sided pattern was activated with the same frequency. Uniform hammering, the paper argued, was the easy input for the in-DRAM frequent-item estimator that TRR implementations relied on. Non-uniform hammering — where each aggressor had its own frequency, phase, and amplitude parameters — generated patterns that estimators with limited tracker capacity could not consistently sample.

The paper formalised non-uniform patterns in a frequency-domain notation. Each aggressor tuple A_k was parametrised by a frequency f (how often within a base period of T accesses the tuple was active), a phase φ (the offset within the base period at which the tuple's activations began), and an amplitude û (the number of consecutive accesses within each active slot). The complete pattern was a superposition of multiple aggressor tuples with distinct (f, φ, û) values, embedded in a base period T whose length was itself a fuzzed parameter. The notation imported signal-processing intuition directly: the Rowhammer access pattern was a discrete-time signal, and TRR was a low-pass filter whose blind spots in the frequency domain the attacker was searching for.

The Blacksmith fuzzer implemented this notation as a black-box pattern discovery tool. The fuzzer generated random (f, φ, û) tuples within calibrated parameter ranges, mapped them to DRAM addresses via a separate address-mapping component, JIT-compiled the resulting access sequence with the `asmjit` library, executed the compiled sequence with cache flushes and memory barriers to ensure the loads reached DRAM, and scanned the surrounding rows for bit flips. Patterns that produced flips were retained; their parameter values were used to update probability distributions that the fuzzer sampled from in subsequent rounds. The pipeline was the standard one for feedback-driven fuzzing, applied to memory-access patterns instead of program inputs.

The four observations that anchor the Blacksmith parameter calibration deserve enumeration. Observation O1, established by an early experiment comparing uniform, partially-randomised, and fully-randomised patterns across 40 DIMMs, showed that non-uniform patterns succeed on DIMMs where uniform patterns fail: 20 of the 40 DIMMs produced bit flips only under non-uniform patterns. Observation O2, established by varying the position of an aggressor pair within a refresh interval, showed that aggressor placement near the end of the interval produces dramatically more bit flips than placement at the beginning; the maximum bit-flip count occurred at offset 91 of a 100-access interval, with offsets 0 to 76 producing zero flips. Observation O3, established by varying the consecutive-repetition intensity of an aggressor pair, showed that bit-flip count exhibits a sweet spot as a function of intensity: too few repetitions and the aggressor does not accumulate enough disturbance; too many and the TRR sampler detects the aggressor and refreshes its neighbours. Observation O4, established by extending patterns beyond a single refresh interval, showed that some DIMMs (notably the B-vendor SK Hynix modules) require patterns spanning multiple refresh intervals to produce flips, with a specific combination of intensity and inter-refresh-interval gap that the fuzzer must search across.

The parameter ranges that emerged from a 24-hour calibration run, and that the paper documents in its Table I, define the "golden set" of fuzzer parameters that enabled the 37-of-40 bit-flip rate on default settings. The number of aggressor tuples per pattern ranges from 8 to 96; the number of refresh intervals per pattern ranges from 1 to 16; the number of aggressors per tuple is either 1 or 2; the base period T ranges from 4 to the pattern length N; the phase φ ranges from 1 to N; the amplitude û ranges from 1 to the activation budget per refresh interval (ACTtREF), or up to 4×ACTtREF for the special-case B-vendor modules. The parameter ranges are conservatively large; the fuzzer learns to concentrate its sampling on the effective sub-ranges via the parameter-tracking feedback mechanism.

The evaluation scope was 40 DDR4 DIMMs and 19 LPDDR4X devices, the former tested on an Intel i7-8700K platform with 12 hours of fuzzing per DIMM and the latter on an in-house JEDEC-compliant development board at 1.5 GHz. The headline result: 40 of 40 DDR4 DIMMs produced bit flips. The comparable TRRespass figure on the same test set was 15 of 40. Blacksmith produced, on average, 87 times more bit flips per DIMM than TRRespass. Of the 40 DIMMs, 32 were exploitable for the PTE attack, 30 for RSA-2048 key corruption, and 15 for the sudo binary attack. The 15 DIMMs vulnerable to all three exploits exceeded the total number of DIMMs that TRRespass had flipped at all.

The qualitative change from TRRespass to Blacksmith is more significant than the quantitative improvement suggests. TRRespass produced single-digit bit-flip counts on most of the 13 modules it flipped; exploitation on those modules was borderline, requiring multiple templating campaigns and luck in flip location. Blacksmith produced bit-flip counts in the hundreds to hundred-thousands across all 40 modules; exploitation was routine, with multiple exploitable flips per templating campaign and exploitation times measured in seconds to minutes. The shift from "proof of concept" to "operational primitive" — the framing the Blacksmith paper uses for its own contribution — is supported by the data: the 87-fold improvement in average bit-flip count corresponds to a similar improvement in templating efficiency and a similar reduction in end-to-end exploitation time.

The LPDDR4X coverage was 16 of 19 devices at default fuzzer parameters. Three devices required parameter extension and an additional reverse-engineering step before yielding flips. The extension was instructive: the three devices were Samsung 2020-production parts whose TRR period was 48 refresh intervals, outside Blacksmith's default parameter range of 1 to 16 refresh intervals per pattern. With the parameter range extended to 36 to 60 refresh intervals and a refresh-aware execution mode enabled, all three devices produced bit flips. The methodological point — that black-box fuzzing is incomplete on its own and benefits from grey-box reverse engineering — anticipated the U-TRR work (Hassan et al., MICRO 2021) and the later Phoenix methodology.

The LPDDR4X substrate proved to be substantially weaker than the DDR4 substrate in the Blacksmith characterisation. The bit-flip counts on LPDDR4X devices were two orders of magnitude larger than those on DDR4 modules tested under the same methodology: the C3 LPDDR4X device (a Vendor C 2018 production part) produced 12.242 million bit flips in a 16 MB sweep; the A3 device produced 1.633 million; the C4 device produced 3.125 million. The two-order-of-magnitude gap reflects the more aggressive die-area and power optimisations that mobile DRAM applies to reduce cost and power consumption at the expense of substrate margin. The mobile ecosystem is, on this evidence, fundamentally more vulnerable to Rowhammer than the desktop ecosystem, despite the prevalence of ECC in mobile DRAM that classical wisdom held to be a partial mitigation.

The frequency-domain formalism became the standard pattern-description language for the post-2022 attack literature. ZenHammer and Phoenix both extend it; PressHammer (Juffinger et al., DIMVA 2024) composes it with the Half-Double distance axis; SledgeHammer (USENIX Security 2024) extends it across bank-level parallelism.

The structural relationship between Blacksmith and Half-Double bears separate observation. The two papers were developed concurrently, with Half-Double announced as a Google security blog post in May 2021 and Blacksmith disclosed in November 2021. Neither paper cites the other in its preprinted form. The two contributions are orthogonal along complementary axes: Blacksmith varies the temporal structure of the access pattern (frequency, phase, amplitude), Half-Double varies the spatial structure (distance from the victim). Both break the assumption that the corresponding axis is a fixed parameter of the attack. The post-2022 attack literature has tended to compose the two axes — the PressHammer construction that combines RowPress with Half-Double, the SledgeHammer construction that distributes Blacksmith-style patterns across banks, the Phoenix construction that adapts the Blacksmith-style frequency-domain notation to the longer DDR5 sampling periods. The compositional structure suggests that the attack space is multidimensional and that the dimensions can be searched independently before being combined.

The Blacksmith TRR characterisation, treated as a side product of the attack methodology, produced the largest-scale empirical TRR survey published to that date. Across 40 DIMMs, the paper inferred sampler sizes ranging from 4 to 28 rows, with vendor-specific clustering: Samsung modules concentrated at the larger end of the range, Micron modules at the smaller. The address-dependence finding — that approximately 55% of the surveyed DIMMs exhibited TRR behaviour sensitive to the specific row addresses of the aggressor rows — established that the in-DRAM sampler was not merely a frequency counter but had some form of address-keyed storage, with implementation details that varied across vendors. The chip-dependence finding — that the bit-flip distribution across the eight DRAM chips of a rank exhibited byte-offset asymmetries on approximately 65% of DIMMs — established that different chips within the same rank, despite operating in lockstep, had distinguishable TRR behaviour. The combined characterisation provided enough material to argue that any unified theoretical model of in-DRAM TRR had to accommodate substantial cross-vendor and cross-chip variation.

### The distance physics

The mechanism by which the wordline voltage of an activated row affects neighbouring cells is, at the device level, a combination of capacitive coupling through the gate dielectric of adjacent cells and electron migration through subthreshold leakage paths. Both mechanisms depend on the physical distance between the activated wordline and the neighbouring cell, with the dependence falling off according to a power-law that has been characterised in the device-physics literature (Walker et al., IEEE TED 2021) as proportional to distance to a power between −1 and −2. The implication is that the distance-1 effect dominates by approximately one order of magnitude over the distance-2 effect under classical Rowhammer hammering parameters, which is why the distance-1 assumption held in the 2014-to-2021 literature.

The Half-Double mechanism does not directly violate the distance physics; it composes the weaker distance-2 effect with the activations contributed by the TRR inhibitor. The far aggressor F contributes some distance-2 disturbance to the victim V, on the order of one-tenth of what a distance-1 aggressor would contribute under the same activation budget. The near aggressor N, when activated by TRR, contributes distance-1 disturbance to V. The total disturbance is the sum, dominated by the TRR-contributed distance-1 component. The attacker controls the F activation rate, which determines the TRR mitigation rate, which determines the N activation rate. The composition is multiplicative in attacker effort: a small number of TRR-induced N activations, each contributing more disturbance per activation than F at the same rate, is sufficient when combined with the F activations to flip V. The distance-2 effect of F is necessary but not sufficient — without it, TRR does not activate N — and the distance-1 effect of N via TRR is necessary but not sufficient — without F, TRR does not activate N enough times to flip V.

The post-Half-Double literature has generalised the distance question. Lang et al. (Blaster, DRAMSec 2023) introduced the concept of "blast radius" to describe the multi-distance disturbance profile of a single activation: rather than a single distance-1 or distance-2 effect, an activation produces a profile of disturbance across multiple distances, with the profile shape depending on the technology node and the activation parameters. The blast-radius framing suggests that distance is not a binary parameter (distance-1 versus distance-2) but a continuous variable, and that attacks targeting any specific distance are sampling from the disturbance profile of the activation. Subsequent academic work — PressHammer, the Luo et al. asymmetric-pattern characterisation — has used the blast-radius framing implicitly, varying the distance parameter alongside the temporal-pattern parameters in fuzzing campaigns.

### The disclosure pattern of the 2021-2022 cluster

The disclosure timelines of Half-Double and Blacksmith together exhibit a pattern that recurs through the Rowhammer literature and deserves separate observation. Half-Double was announced publicly via the Google Security Blog in May 2021; the academic paper appeared at USENIX Security 2022 in August 2022, after a fifteen-month embargo coordinated with the DRAM and SoC vendors. The blog announcement contained only a high-level description of the phenomenon; the precise mechanism (TRR co-option) was not described until the paper appeared. The embargo was driven by contractual obligations among the paper's author set, which included Google security engineers (subject to vendor NDAs) alongside academic researchers at Graz TU. The Blacksmith disclosure began in Q1 2021 through the Swiss National Cybersecurity Centre (NCSC-CH), proceeded through a Q2 2021 vendor notification phase, and concluded with public disclosure on 15 November 2021. The IEEE S&P 2022 paper appeared in May 2022. The Blacksmith embargo period was approximately nine months; CVE-2021-42114 was assigned during the embargo.

The two embargo periods overlapped — Blacksmith's public disclosure preceded Half-Double's by approximately nine months — but the disclosure mechanisms differed. Half-Double was disclosed via vendor coordination, with Google as the coordination lead and the academic paper held until after the vendor remediation cycle had completed. Blacksmith was disclosed via national CERT coordination (NCSC-CH), with public disclosure on a calendar date independent of vendor remediation status. The two patterns reflect different theories of how disclosure should align with mitigation: Google's pattern treats public disclosure as the endpoint of a process that includes vendor remediation, while NCSC's pattern treats public disclosure as an event independent of vendor response. Neither approach has produced public commitment to specific mitigation deployment timelines from the affected DRAM vendors; Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron have not, as of public information, made disclosed statements about Rowhammer mitigation roadmaps in response to either paper.

The recurrence of this pattern through the Rowhammer literature is part of why the academic measurements have value beyond their immediate technical content. The vendor side of the Rowhammer story is conducted in private: SPD MAC field configurations, in-DRAM TRR implementation choices, and mitigation deployment schedules are not in the public domain. The academic measurements are the primary source of empirical information about what vendors have deployed and how it performs. The CVE numbering for Rowhammer attacks reflects this structure — CVE-2020-10255 for TRRespass, CVE-2021-42114 for Blacksmith, CVE-2025-6202 for Phoenix — even though Rowhammer is not a software vulnerability in the conventional sense. The CVEs serve as bibliographic anchors for the academic literature rather than as references to specific vendor products.

**Assumption invalidated.** The implicit assumption of the 2014 to 2021 literature was that aggressors in a many-sided Rowhammer pattern should be hammered with equal frequency, on the grounds that maximum total activation produced maximum disturbance. Blacksmith falsifies this assumption: non-uniform patterns produce more disturbance per total-activation budget when the mitigation is a streaming top-k estimator, because non-uniform inputs are systematically harder for such estimators to summarise correctly. Half-Double falsifies a different uniformity assumption: that the spatial structure of the Rowhammer effect is fixed at distance 1. The two papers together establish that both the temporal and the spatial structure of the attack are search variables.

## §5 DDR5 and the Phoenix Result

The 2021 introduction of DDR5 was accompanied by a vendor narrative of improved Rowhammer resistance. The narrative had three substantive components: an improved in-DRAM TRR with larger sampler memory, the introduction of Refresh Management (RFM) commands in the JEDEC DDR5 spec for memory-controller-coordinated mitigation, and the standardisation of on-die single-bit ECC. Between 2021 and 2024 the public literature contained no end-to-end DDR5 Rowhammer exploit. The closest result was a single bit flip on 1 of 10 DDR5 modules in the ZenHammer evaluation (USENIX Security 2024). The community read this as evidence that DDR5 was, in practical terms, resistant.

The Phoenix paper, by Meyer, Jattke, Marazzi, Qazi, Moghimi, and Razavi at IEEE S&P 2026, refutes that reading. Two parallel results from 2025 are relevant context. REFault (Gloor et al., µASC 2025) measured DDR5 HCmin directly with the periodic-refresh-disabled methodology and found values in the 32,000 range across Samsung and Micron parts — comparable to the DDR4 substrate or worse. McSee (Jattke et al., USENIX Security 2025) instrumented production memory controllers with an oscilloscope and showed that AMD Zen 4, Intel Alder Lake, and Intel Raptor Lake do not issue RFM commands under Rowhammer-class workloads, regardless of the JEDEC specification. Two of the three components of the DDR5 narrative — improved substrate, RFM use in practice — were therefore already falsified before Phoenix appeared. Phoenix attacked the remaining component, the improved in-DRAM TRR, directly.

The Phoenix methodology contained three structural pieces.

The first piece was a refresh-interval-granularity reverse engineering of the SK Hynix DDR5 TRR mechanism. The U-TRR per-ACT methodology of Hassan et al. (MICRO 2021) did not scale to DDR5 because the TRR sampling period had grown from 16 tREFI in DDR4 to 128 tREFI in the first SK Hynix DDR5 family (designated H2 in the paper) and to 2608 tREFI in the second family (H6). A per-ACT brute force over 2608 × 59 = 153,872 ACT slots was computationally infeasible. The paper's reverse-engineering protocol was a zoom-out, zoom-in approach: first identify the sampling period N by running the experiment for many candidate Ns and observing which period produced a stable refresh histogram; second, identify within each period the "lightly sampled" intervals where TRR's effective sampling rate was near zero; third, identify within each lightly sampled interval the specific ACT slot positions at which TRR did sample, so that decoys could be placed at those positions and real aggressors hidden elsewhere.

The second piece was the construction of two attack patterns, P128 and P2608, tailored to the H2 and H6 sampling periods respectively. Each pattern alternated decoy hammering (to satisfy the heavily sampled portion of the TRR window) with aggressor hammering (to drive the actual disturbance). Both patterns were strictly JEDEC DDR5-compliant: no timing parameter was violated, no nonstandard command was issued. The patterns produced bit flips only when the start of execution aligned with a specific subset of REF counter offsets — 2 of 128 for P128, 92 of 2608 for P2608. The alignment probability was raised to 25 to 56 percent by combining multiple shifted pattern instances and bank-level parallelism.

The third piece was a self-correcting synchronisation mechanism. The ZenHammer continuous-non-repeating REF detection routine, which worked on Zen 3 with DDR4, exhibited a 0.125% error rate per refresh interval on Zen 4 with DDR5 — small per interval, but compounded over the 8,000+ intervals required to reach HCmin into a survival probability of approximately 0.000169% at 40 ACTs/tREFI. The paper's epistemic move was to abandon the attempt to detect every REF and instead to detect missed REFs after the fact: by comparing the `rdtscp` timestamp delta between successive detections against a threshold of 1.4× tREFI, the synchronisation logic recognised when one or more REFs had been missed and advanced the pattern execution state by the corresponding number of intervals. The error rate under this scheme was zero in the evaluated traces. The general pattern — accept noise, correct downstream — is borrowed structurally from TCP retransmission and filesystem journaling, and is now part of the standard Rowhammer toolkit.

The DDR5 on-die ECC (ODECC) component of the substrate deserves separate attention because its interaction with Rowhammer-class attacks is non-trivial. ODECC corrects single-bit errors silently within each ECC codeword, typically a 128-bit word with 8 parity bits providing single-error-correction, double-error-detection (SECDED). The corrected value is returned to the requester, but — and this is the consequential detail — the corrected value is not written back to the DRAM cell. A bit flip caused by Rowhammer, once it has occurred, persists in the cell across refresh windows. A second bit flip in the same codeword, accumulated across multiple refresh windows, exhausts the correction capacity and produces an uncorrectable error that the memory controller observes. From the attacker's perspective, ODECC turns single-bit-flip exploits into two-bit-flip exploits within the same codeword, which is a moderate increase in difficulty rather than a categorical barrier. The Phoenix exploit on DIMM H11 demonstrates this in practice: the bit-flip budget required for a successful PTE attack is approximately double that which would be required without ODECC, but the budget is still reachable within minutes of templating.

The Phoenix vulnerable-offset analysis bears separate consideration because it illustrates the tension between attack reliability and attack reach. The P128 pattern produces bit flips only when execution begins at one of 2 specific REFRESH counter offsets out of 128 — a 1.56% alignment probability under uncontrolled execution. The P2608 pattern requires alignment to one of 92 offsets out of 2608, a 3.53% probability. On the FPGA platform, the alignment is controlled directly: the attacker issues extra REFRESH commands until the counter reaches the desired offset. On the commodity platform, no such control exists, and the attacker must execute the pattern repeatedly and discard the runs that do not align. The paper's optimisations — running four shifted pattern instances in parallel, exploiting bank-level parallelism with synchronised offset shifts across banks — raise the effective alignment probability to approximately 25% for P128 and 56% for P2608 per execution attempt. The 100% end-to-end success rate over 10 attack runs reflects the compounding of multiple attempts within the templating campaign, not single-shot success.

The cross-device table from the Phoenix paper is informative for understanding production-DDR5 substrate heterogeneity. Of the 15 DDR5 UDIMMs tested, the most vulnerable (DIMM H11, January 2024 production) produced approximately 12,500 1-to-0 bit flips and 18,500 0-to-1 flips per 256 MB sweep with the P2608 pattern, with a best-case PTE attack time of 6 seconds. The least vulnerable that still produced bit flips (DIMM H14, December 2024 production) produced 24 1-to-0 flips and 19 0-to-1 flips with the P2608 pattern, with a PTE attack time of 20 minutes 15 seconds. The four-order-of-magnitude variation in bit-flip count and three-order-of-magnitude variation in exploitation time within a single vendor's product line, across modules manufactured a year apart, indicates that the substrate manufacturing process is not stationary across production runs. The variation does not, by itself, indicate which production runs are more or less vulnerable; characterising any specific module requires the templating campaign that the exploit itself performs.

The evaluation scope was 10 SK Hynix DDR5 RDIMMs on the FPGA platform and 15 SK Hynix DDR5 UDIMMs on a commodity AMD Zen 4 platform (Ryzen 7 7700X, Ubuntu 20.04, default BIOS). The result on the commodity platform: 15 of 15 DIMMs produced bit flips. Each DIMM was vulnerable to exactly one of P128 or P2608 — eight to the former, seven to the latter. The end-to-end PTE exploit, integrated with the Rubicon framework (Bölcskei et al., EuroS&P 2025), reached root on DIMM H11 in an average of 5 minutes and 19 seconds across ten runs, with a best case of 1 minute 49 seconds and a 100% success rate. The headline single-run figure of 109 seconds is the best case.

The Phoenix paper does not claim coverage of Samsung or Micron DDR5; the authors estimate that the reverse-engineering effort would be a half person-year per additional vendor. Nor does it cover Intel CPUs, on which the self-correcting synchronisation would need to be re-tuned and on which the in-CPU pTRR mechanism (characterised separately by McSee) presents an additional bypass requirement. The paper's research-agenda section names all three of these gaps explicitly.

### The Phoenix end-to-end timing

The end-to-end timing of the Phoenix exploit on DIMM H11 illustrates the structure of a modern Rowhammer attack against a hardened target. The attack begins with the allocation of a 4 MiB physically-contiguous memory block via the Linux buddy allocator. The buddy allocator's behaviour under exhaustion produces contiguous regions; the attacker observes the allocation pattern via the row-conflict timing side channel and selects the contiguous region for the attack. The allocation phase typically takes a few seconds.

The next phase is DRAM addressing reverse engineering. The ZenHammer-reverse-engineered DDR5 addressing functions for the Zen 4 platform are used directly, without per-attack re-derivation; the addressing functions are platform-static and need only be recovered once per (CPU, motherboard, BIOS) combination. The bank-conflict patterns within the contiguous region are measured and used to identify the rows that are at distance 1 and distance 2 from the candidate aggressor positions.

The templating phase places aggressor data (0xAA repeated) and surrogate-victim data (a known PTE value) in the appropriate rows. The Phoenix pattern (P128 or P2608, depending on the DIMM family) is executed; the self-correcting synchronisation routine maintains alignment across the thousands of REFRESH intervals required to reach HCmin. Bit flips are detected by checking whether the victim data has been corrupted; the location of flips is recorded.

The exploitation phase, using the Rubicon framework, installs target PTE data at the templated flip locations. The PTE attack on H11 flips a bit in the Physical Frame Number of a target PTE, redirecting a virtual page to a different physical frame. The redirected frame is one that holds another PTE in the kernel's page-table hierarchy. The attacker maps the redirected frame and gains read/write access to the kernel page table. With kernel page-table R/W, root privilege is straightforward.

The total end-to-end time on DIMM H11 across 10 runs ranged from 1 minute 49 seconds (best case) to 17 minutes 6 seconds (worst case), with an average of 5 minutes 19 seconds. The variance reflects the alignment-probability variance in pattern execution: in the best case, the pattern aligns with a vulnerable REFRESH offset on the first attempt; in the worst case, multiple attempts are needed before alignment is achieved. The 100% success rate across the 10 runs reflects the fact that the attack is designed to retry on misalignment, accepting variable time-to-success in exchange for reliable success.

### The McSee oscilloscope measurement

The McSee paper (Jattke et al., USENIX Security 2025) deserves separate description because it produced the first public empirical characterisation of memory-controller behaviour under Rowhammer-class workloads, by direct electrical measurement on the DDR5 command bus. The methodology connects an oscilloscope to the command pins of a DDR5 DIMM (a non-trivial physical setup requiring custom probe boards) and records the sequence of commands issued by the memory controller during attacker-driven memory access patterns. The recorded command traces are decoded into ACT, PRE, REF, RD, WR, and RFM commands; the distribution of RFM commands under sustained Rowhammer-class activity is the security-relevant measurement.

The McSee finding, as summarised in §5 of this writeup, is that AMD Zen 4, Intel Alder Lake, and Intel Raptor Lake memory controllers do not issue RFM commands under Rowhammer-class workloads, regardless of the JEDEC DDR5 specification's provision for them. The implication is that the memory-controller-side counter-based mitigation that JEDEC specified is not active in commodity DDR5 deployments. The reason is not stated explicitly in the public literature; vendor statements have not been issued. Two plausible explanations are that the memory-controller firmware does not implement the RFM counter logic, or that the firmware implements it with a threshold high enough that Rowhammer-class workloads do not trigger it. The McSee methodology cannot distinguish between these two explanations; both would produce the observed empirical absence.

The methodological contribution of McSee extends beyond the specific RFM finding. The oscilloscope methodology produces ground truth about memory-controller behaviour that no software-side observation can. The DRAM command bus is the contract between the memory controller and the DRAM, and any mitigation that is supposed to occur on the bus is observable on the bus. Future characterisation of PRAC Alert behaviour, of RFM scheduling policies, and of memory-controller responses to ABO state transitions can proceed via the same methodology. The cost is the hardware setup; the benefit is unambiguous data.

**Assumption invalidated.** The 2021 to 2024 working assumption was that DDR5's combined defences — improved in-DRAM TRR, RFM commands, on-die ECC — provided practical immunity. Phoenix falsifies the in-DRAM TRR component directly. The McSee and REFault results falsify the RFM and substrate components independently. The combined picture is that none of the three components of the DDR5 narrative was supported by experiment when the experiments were finally performed.

## §6 RowPress — The Row-Open Duration Axis

The Rowhammer literature between 2014 and 2022 treated access count as the sole independent variable of the disturbance phenomenon. Pattern parameters — number of aggressors, spatial layout, frequency, phase, amplitude — all decomposed into modulations of how often a given row was activated within a refresh window. The duration that a row remained open per activation, parameterised as tAggON, was held implicitly at the JEDEC minimum tRAS of approximately 36 nanoseconds.

In 2023, Luo, Olgun, Yağlıkçı, Tuğrul, Rhyner, Cavlak, Lindegger, Sadrosadati, and Mutlu published "RowPress: Amplifying Read Disturbance in Modern DRAM Chips" at the 50th International Symposium on Computer Architecture (ISCA 2023). The paper received the conference's Distinguished Artifact Award. Its scope was 164 DDR4 chips from Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron across 12 die revisions, characterised on a Xilinx Alveo U200 FPGA running DRAM Bender (Olgun et al., 2022) with temperature held under PID control between 50 and 80 degrees Celsius.

The paper's central result is the discovery that holding a row open for an extended duration — orders of magnitude longer than the tRAS minimum — produces bit flips by a mechanism distinct from classical Rowhammer. The measured tAggON sensitivity is sharp: at 7.8 µs of row-open time (one tREFI), the minimum activation count for a bit flip is reduced by approximately 17.6× on average across vendors relative to the 36 ns baseline; at 70.2 µs (nine tREFIs, the maximum allowed under DDR4 refresh postponement), the reduction is 159× on average; at 30 ms, a single activation suffices to flip approximately 13.1% of tested rows.

The phenomenon is anatomically distinct from Rowhammer in four respects. First, the cells that flip under RowPress conditions overlap by less than 0.013% with the cells that flip under classical Rowhammer conditions on the same chip; the two failure modes target different cell populations. Second, the bit-flip directionality is inverted: classical Rowhammer is dominated by 0-to-1 flips in true-cell layouts, RowPress is dominated by 1-to-0 flips at large tAggON, with the transition occurring smoothly as tAggON increases. Third, the access pattern preferences invert: classical double-sided patterns produce more flips than single-sided at small tAggON, but single-sided patterns become more effective at tAggON above approximately 1.5 µs. Fourth, the temperature dependence is monotonic rather than non-monotonic: RowPress always worsens with increasing temperature within the tested 50-to-80°C range, whereas classical Rowhammer has a complex non-monotonic temperature response (Orosa et al., MICRO 2021).

The physical interpretation offered in the paper, drawing on a concurrent Samsung arXiv preprint, identifies the underlying mechanism as a "passing gate effect" in which the elevated wordline voltage of the activated row, sustained for tAggON, attracts electrons from neighbouring cells through subthreshold leakage paths. Classical Rowhammer is a charge-injection mechanism — adjacent cells gain charge from repeated activation transients; RowPress is a charge-extraction mechanism — adjacent cells lose charge under sustained wordline elevation. The opposite flip directionality follows directly from the opposite direction of charge movement.

The paper documents that industry was aware of the phenomenon before the academic disclosure. Micron US patents from 2014 and 2018 describe a "RAS Clobber" effect with substantively the same physical signature; Samsung's contemporaneous arXiv preprint uses the "passing gate effect" naming. The decade-long gap between the patent disclosure and the academic publication recapitulates the obscurity pattern of the TRR mechanism: the phenomenon was protected by defensive intellectual property and marketing-safe naming rather than by absence.

The numeric data that the RowPress paper produces are sharp enough to merit reproduction. At tAggON = 36 ns (the tRAS minimum), the average ACmin across vendors matches the published TRR-bypass Rowhammer thresholds. At tAggON = 186 ns, the ACmin reduces by approximately 1.04× to 1.17× across vendors — a modest effect. At tAggON = 7.8 µs (one tREFI), the reduction is 21× on average, with Samsung 8 Gb B-Die parts showing a 59× reduction. At tAggON = 70.2 µs (nine tREFIs), the reduction is 190× on average, with the same Samsung parts showing a 537× reduction. At tAggON = 30 ms (half of a refresh window), a single activation suffices to flip a bit in 13.1% of tested rows on the most vulnerable die revisions. The relationship between ACmin and tAggON is approximately log-linear with a slope of −1.0 across all three vendors, suggesting that the underlying physical mechanism is the same across vendors and produces a reciprocal trade-off: ACmin × tAggONmin ≈ constant for any given row.

The temperature dependence of RowPress is monotonic in the tested 50-to-80°C range. At 80°C and tAggON = 7.8 µs, the ACmin reduction relative to 36 ns is 48× on average across vendors, compared to 21× at 50°C. The fraction of rows that produce bit flips at large tAggON approaches 100% across all vendor product lines at 80°C, including the SK Hynix 4 Gb A-Die parts that are immune to RowPress at 50°C. The implication for production environments is significant: server and datacenter installations operate at chip temperatures in the 60-to-70°C range under typical loads and at 80°C or higher under heavy loads, well within the temperature regime that maximises RowPress sensitivity. Lab characterisations performed at 50°C systematically underestimate the production-environment RowPress threat.

The real-system demonstration in §6 of the paper used an Intel i5-10400 with Samsung DDR4 under the vendor's in-DRAM TRR mitigation. The user-level test program exploited an indirect mechanism for raising tAggON: by accessing 16 distinct cache blocks within a single row in sequence, the program caused the memory controller to keep the row open across the entire sequence in order to serve the subsequent accesses from the open row buffer. The implicit tAggON increase, combined with the 16-dummy-row TRR bypass from Gruss et al. (S&P 2018), produced 258 bit flips in 191 rows where the same program executing a classical Rowhammer pattern produced 0 flips.

The mitigation discussion in §7 of the paper is candid about the limitations of retrofitting existing counter-based defences. Hard tRAS limits are costly to row-buffer locality. Doubled refresh rates remain ineffective at tAggON > tREFI and impose bandwidth overhead. Per-row activation counters extended with tRAS-aware threshold adjustment achieve a reasonable trade-off in simulation: PARA with the extension shows 3.6% average slowdown on SPEC CPU 2017, Graphene with the extension shows a marginal speedup. None of these is presented as a long-term solution. The long-term solution implied by the paper is a substrate-level redesign that includes explicit tAggON tracking alongside per-row activation counting, which the DDR5 JESD79-5C specification does not yet require.

The inversion of the canonical Rowhammer wisdom regarding single-sided versus double-sided patterns is one of the most consequential of the RowPress findings for mitigation design. Classical Rowhammer wisdom, established by Kim et al. (2014) and unchallenged through 2022, holds that double-sided patterns produce more bit flips than single-sided patterns under the same activation budget, because the victim row is stressed from both sides of its physical layout. The RowPress paper measures the opposite for large tAggON: at tAggON > 1.5 µs on the most vulnerable Samsung parts, single-sided patterns produce 4,210 fewer required activations than double-sided patterns; at 80°C, the difference grows to 8,699 fewer activations. The inversion is structural rather than statistical: at large tAggON, the dominant disturbance mechanism is the wordline voltage gradient that the open-row state produces, which is a single-row property; a second aggressor on the other side of the victim does not contribute additional disturbance proportional to its activation count. The implication for mitigation is that TRR mechanisms optimised for detecting double-sided or many-sided patterns are blind to single-sided RowPress; a row that is activated infrequently but held open for long durations escapes the TRR sampler's frequency-based filter.

The post-RowPress literature has extended the read-disturbance phenomenon to additional axes. PressHammer (Juffinger et al., DIMVA 2024) composes RowPress with Half-Double, producing a distance-2 attack at extended tAggON. Luo et al. (2024) characterise asymmetric double-sided patterns in which one aggressor is hammered and the other is pressed. Yağlıkçı et al. (MICRO 2024) introduce wordline voltage as a third axis, showing that reducing the wordline voltage reduces both the classical and the RowPress failure modes proportionally. The two-dimensional model — access count × row-open duration — is therefore already in process of being generalised to three dimensions.

The RowPress characterisation contains a finer technology-scaling observation that bears separate mention. Within each vendor's product line, the fraction of rows that exhibit any RowPress bit flip at large tAggON is monotonically increasing with die revision. Samsung's 8 Gb B-Die (2020 production) shows under 60% row coverage at the largest tested tAggON; the C-Die shows under 80%; the D-Die shows approximately 100% — every row in the test region produced at least one flip. Similar trends appear for SK Hynix and Micron product lines. The pattern is consistent with the technology-scaling explanation of decreasing HCmin in classical Rowhammer: smaller cell capacitors, denser packing, and thinner wordline isolation all push the RowPress threshold downward in parallel with the Rowhammer threshold. Extrapolation to DDR5 — which the RowPress paper does not directly test — projects that the row-coverage fraction at large tAggON should approach 100% across vendors. The implication is that any RowPress-aware mitigation deployed in DDR5 must address a substrate population in which essentially every cell is, in principle, vulnerable.

The real-system demonstration in §6 of the RowPress paper merits closer examination because it identifies a specific memory-controller behaviour as the lever for inducing extended tAggON from unprivileged user code. Modern memory controllers implement an open-row policy that keeps a row in the row buffer for some duration after the last access, on the expectation that subsequent accesses will exhibit spatial locality and benefit from the open-row hit. The duration that the row remains open is controlled by the memory controller's row-buffer-management policy, which varies by vendor and by configuration. By issuing multiple loads to different cache blocks within the same row in rapid sequence, the user-level program signals to the memory controller that the row is being used and induces the controller to leave the row open longer. The 30-cycle gap between the first cache-block access (which incurs the row-activation cost) and subsequent accesses (which hit the open row buffer) is empirically detectable via the Intel timestamp counter, confirming that the row remains open for the duration of the sequence. The NUM_READS parameter — the number of cache blocks accessed per row activation — is the attacker's control over tAggON, with the optimum on the tested platform being approximately 16 cache blocks per activation.

The non-monotonic relationship between NUM_READS and bit-flip count on the tested platform is itself anatomically interesting. At NUM_READS = 1, the program executes a classical Rowhammer pattern and produces 8 bit flips. At NUM_READS = 16, the program produces 258 flips in 191 rows. At NUM_READS = 48, the program produces only 2 flips. The non-monotonicity arises from a balance between two competing effects: increasing NUM_READS increases tAggON and therefore the RowPress contribution to disturbance, but it also reduces the activation frequency of the real aggressor rows relative to the 16-dummy-row TRR-bypass technique, allowing TRR to track and refresh the real aggressors' neighbours. The optimum is platform-specific and configuration-specific. The implication for any defensive deployment is that monitoring NUM_READS alone is insufficient — the relevant quantity is the combined access count and row-open duration profile per row per refresh interval.

### The retention-failure axis

A separate axis of read disturbance, mentioned briefly in the original Kim et al. paper and characterised in subsequent work, is retention failure: a cell that has been undisturbed since its last refresh may flip if the refresh window is too long for the cell's retention time. The Kim et al. paper observed that retention-failure-vulnerable cells (those with sub-64-millisecond retention times) overlapped with Rowhammer-vulnerable cells at only about 5%. The RowPress paper extends this analysis: retention-failure cells overlap with RowPress cells at less than 0.34%. The three failure populations — Rowhammer, RowPress, retention failure — are largely distinct, each occupying a different cell subpopulation within the same module.

The retention-failure axis is normally suppressed by the refresh mechanism: every cell is refreshed every 64 ms (DDR4) or 32 ms (DDR5), which keeps retention failures below the noise floor for cells with retention times longer than the window. Attacks that suppress or delay refresh — by issuing refresh-postpone commands within the JEDEC-permitted budget, or by exploiting specific memory-controller-side timing — can expose retention failures as a separate vector. The U-TRR paper (Hassan et al., MICRO 2021) uses retention-failure-profiled canary rows as a side channel to detect TRR-induced refreshes, which is itself an application of the retention-failure axis to attack instrumentation rather than to bit-flip exploitation.

### The methodological export of the SAFARI group

The RowPress paper, like the broader Mutlu-group output of which it is part, is a characterisation paper rather than an attack paper. The structural template of the genre is consistent across the group's output: large chip count (164 chips for RowPress), multi-vendor coverage (Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron), parameter sweep exhaustiveness (9 tAggON values, 7 data patterns, 7 temperatures, multiple die revisions), FPGA-based infrastructure for cycle-accurate control, open-source data and code release, numbered observations (Observations 1 through 21 in RowPress), and a small set of takeaways that summarise the architectural-implication-level conclusions. The template defines the characterisation-paper genre in DRAM security; subsequent papers — ZenHammer, Phoenix, the various U-TRR descendants — follow it closely.

The pedagogical export of the template is itself significant. Graduate students reading the characterisation papers learn a research methodology in addition to the specific findings: how to scope a characterisation study, how to design parameter sweeps that produce both positive and negative results, how to structure numbered observations that subsequent work can cite, and how to release data in formats that support reproducibility. The methodological export probably matters more for the long-term trajectory of the field than any specific characterisation finding; the field is still adding axes (wordline voltage, refresh-timing patterns) and the methodology that the existing papers exemplify is the template that the new-axis papers will follow.

The SAFARI open-source releases — SoftMC, DRAM Bender, the RowPress artifact bundle that won the ISCA 2023 Distinguished Artifact Award — are the practical instruments of the methodology. SoftMC and DRAM Bender are FPGA-based memory-controller substrates that researchers can extend for new DRAM generations or new characterisation studies. The published artifacts include source code, raw measurement data, simulation infrastructure, and plotting scripts. The cumulative effect over a decade is that the entry cost for new DRAM characterisation research is now bounded by FPGA hardware availability and graduate-student time rather than by tooling development.

**Assumption invalidated.** The 2014 to 2022 working assumption was that read disturbance in DRAM was a one-dimensional phenomenon parameterised by activation count. RowPress falsifies this assumption: the row-open duration is an independent axis with its own threshold structure, its own cell population, its own bit-flip directionality, and its own temperature response. Mitigations that monitor only the activation-count axis are systematically blind to attacks that operate primarily along the duration axis.

## §7 ZenHammer and the AMD Side

Between 2014 and 2024, public Rowhammer evaluations were dominated by Intel platforms. The original Kim et al. paper had tested both Intel and AMD Piledriver systems and reported bit flips on both; the post-2014 academic literature, including TRRespass, Blacksmith, Half-Double, and the various end-to-end exploitation papers, used Intel hosts. A 2016 comparative study by Lanteigne reported approximately 59 bit flips on Piledriver versus 22,900 on Haswell, a two-order-of-magnitude gap that contributed to a community narrative of AMD resistance. AMD's BIOS and Kernel Developer's Guide stopped documenting DRAM addressing functions with the introduction of the Zen architecture, complicating the experimental setup further.

ZenHammer, by Jattke, Wipfli, Solt, Marazzi, Bölcskei, and Razavi at USENIX Security 2024, falsifies the resistance narrative. The paper's core finding is that Rowhammer on AMD Zen platforms had been absent from the literature not because of platform-level immunity but because the existing tooling — DRAM addressing reverse engineering, refresh synchronisation, and activation-rate optimisation — had been implicitly Intel-specific. With three substrate-aware adjustments, all three of the published Intel-platform attacks (PTE, RSA-2048 key, sudo binary) reproduced on Zen 2 and Zen 3 with timing comparable to Intel Coffee Lake.

The three adjustments correspond to three distinct undocumented properties of the Zen substrate.

The first is the DRAM addressing function. DRAMA (Pessl et al., USENIX Security 2016), the standard tool for reverse-engineering address-to-bank/row/column functions, assumes that the function is a pure XOR of physical-address bits. The assumption is correct on Intel platforms. On AMD Zen, the physical address space above 4 GiB is remapped to fill the PCI MMIO hole, with a vendor-specific offset that AMD documented as "Memory Hoisting" up to the pre-Zen Family 15h BKDG and stopped documenting thereafter. The effect is that XOR fits produce systematic inconsistencies on Zen address spaces. The paper's DARE tool extends DRAMA with PCI-range auto-detection from `/proc/iomem` and offset subtraction before XOR fitting; with this preprocessing, the addressing functions become recoverable on Zen+, Zen 2, Zen 3, and Zen 4.

The second is refresh synchronisation. The Blacksmith ref_sync routine uses a tight `rdtscp` / `lfence` / load / flush / `rdtscp` loop to detect REFRESH events via the access-latency spike that REFRESH induces. On Intel Coffee Lake the routine produces a median REF-to-REF interval close to the JEDEC nominal 7.8 µs with 1.1% outliers. On Zen 3 the routine produces a median of 5.37 µs with 93.4% outliers. The substrate cause, identified by the paper, is that Zen's memory controller is free to schedule REFRESH commands during the untimed gap between iterations of the synchronisation loop — the interval that contains the `if`, the branch, and the implicit `lfence` between iterations. The routine therefore misses many of the REFRESHes that occur during these gaps. The paper's continuous-non-repeating fix moves the flush out of the timing loop and uses every `rdtscp` simultaneously as the previous iteration's stop and the next iteration's start, eliminating the untimed gap. With 64 row accesses per iteration, the routine produces a median of 7.77 µs with 0.6% outliers on Zen 3.

The third is activation throughput. Zen 3 produces approximately 37 activations per tREFI under the standard Blacksmith access loop, compared to 76.8 on Coffee Lake. Within an 8192-tREFI refresh window, the lower throughput is insufficient to reach the DDR4 HCmin of approximately 50,000 to 100,000 activations on most modules. The paper grid-searches over flushing strategy (CLFLUSH versus CLFLUSHOPT, gather versus scatter), fencing strategy (MFENCE/LFENCE/SFENCE at various intervals), and access type (loads, stores, non-temporal variants) to identify combinations that maximise activation throughput. The most consequential observation is that on Zen 3, loads following a CLFLUSHOPT to the same cache line never incur cache hits, even without an explicit memory barrier. The AMD64 Architecture Programmer's Manual does not guarantee this ordering — it specifies only that loads are ordered with same-cache-line stores — but the silicon empirically enforces it on Zen 3. The empirical guarantee allows omission of fence instructions between accesses to the same aggressor, which raises throughput.

The DDR4 evaluation tested 10 DIMMs (six Samsung, two SK Hynix, two Micron) on Zen+, Zen 2, Zen 3, and Intel Coffee Lake. Seven of the ten DIMMs produced bit flips on Zen 2; six of ten on Zen 3; eight of ten on Coffee Lake. One DIMM (SK Hynix H0) produced 0 flips on Coffee Lake and 182 on Zen 2, indicating that the Zen-optimised configurations did not merely replicate Intel results but accessed regions of the parameter space that the Intel configurations did not reach. The end-to-end PTE exploit on Ryzen 5 5600G with THP coloring reached root in 93 seconds.

The THP-coloring technique that ZenHammer uses for exploitation deserves separate mention because it generalises a technique introduced for cloud co-tenancy attacks into a single-system primitive. Standard THP allocation guarantees physical-page alignment within a 2 MB region, but the DRAM addressing function uses bits above bit 21 (i.e., bits identifying which 2 MB region) to determine bank, row, and column. Two 2 MB-aligned regions in the same process may therefore map to different banks of the DRAM, making it impossible to use them for the same hammering attack. The coloring technique allocates a 256 MB region of THPs, assigns each THP a "color" based on its high-order physical-address bits (recovered via the row-conflict timing side channel), and groups same-color THPs together. Within a same-color group, the low 21 bits of the physical address are sufficient to identify rows precisely, and hammering across multiple THPs in the same color is feasible. The coloring step itself takes 39 seconds on the tested system; the per-color row detection takes 18 milliseconds. The combined cost is amortised across the entire exploitation campaign, not paid per attack.

The DDR5 evaluation produced 1 bit flip across 10 tested DIMMs, on a single Samsung module. The result was the first reported DDR5 Rowhammer bit flip in the public literature. The paper's discussion treated it as a proof of concept rather than as a practical attack; the subsequent Phoenix work (§5 of this writeup) extended the result to 15 of 15 SK Hynix DDR5 UDIMMs by attacking the in-DRAM TRR mechanism directly.

The ZenHammer DDR5 result, in retrospect, was a tooling-limited measurement rather than a substrate-limited one. The Blacksmith-derived pattern library that ZenHammer used was tuned for DDR4's 16-tREFI sampling period; DDR5's 128-to-2608-tREFI sampling periods (as Phoenix later characterised them) were outside the search space that Blacksmith's pattern generation considered. The 1-of-10 ZenHammer result reflects the probability that a DDR4-tuned pattern, executed on a DDR5 substrate, would accidentally align with a DDR5 vulnerability window. The Phoenix 15-of-15 result reflects the probability that a DDR5-tuned pattern, executed on the same substrate after explicit reverse-engineering of the DDR5 sampling period, would produce flips. The substrate did not change between the two studies; the tooling did. The pattern of community misreading — interpreting a tooling-limited null result as evidence of substrate-level resistance — recurs in the Rowhammer literature. The lesson is that null results in attack papers should be interpreted as upper bounds on the difficulty of finding flips under a specific methodology, not as lower bounds on substrate robustness.

The cross-cutting observation from ZenHammer is methodological. The tooling that an attack paper relies on — addressing-function reverse engineering, REFRESH synchronisation, fence omission — is substrate-coupled. A tool developed on one microarchitecture may fail silently on another. The Blacksmith ref_sync did not produce an error message on Zen 3; it produced wrong answers. The community's reading of those wrong answers as evidence of AMD resistance was structurally unavoidable until someone undertook the substrate-aware re-engineering that ZenHammer documents. The principle generalises: experimental tools in security research have implicit substrate dependencies that the tools themselves do not surface, and porting an attack to a new substrate is itself a research contribution.

The AMD Memory Hoisting offset, identified in §3 of the ZenHammer paper as the Memory Hoisting mechanism that perturbs DRAM addressing-function recovery, is worth describing in more detail because the underlying mechanism is platform-architectural rather than DRAM-architectural. In x86-64 systems, the physical address space is shared between system DRAM and memory-mapped I/O regions (PCI and PCIe device registers, BIOS-reserved regions, IOMMU translation tables). The PCI MMIO region is conventionally placed in the address range immediately below 4 GiB, occupying a configurable hole whose size depends on the system's PCI device population. To recover the physical DRAM that would otherwise be hidden under the MMIO hole, the memory controller remaps the addresses above 4 GiB so that the DRAM physical address space is contiguous from 0 to (total RAM size). The remapping subtracts a constant offset — equal to the size of the MMIO hole below 4 GiB — from the operating-system-visible physical address before applying the DRAM addressing function. Intel platforms have historically documented this remapping under various names ("memory remapping", "OS Invisible Reclaim", "TOLUD/TOUUD configuration"); AMD platforms documented it as "Memory Hoisting" through the Family 15h BKDG and stopped documenting it with the introduction of the Zen architecture. The ZenHammer DARE tool recovers the offset by reading the PCI range from `/proc/iomem` and subtracting it from physical addresses before fitting the XOR addressing function; the fit succeeds when the correct offset is applied and fails when it is not.

The Zen 3 ordering observation — that loads following a CLFLUSHOPT to the same cache line never incur cache hits, without an explicit MFENCE — is documented in the ZenHammer paper as Observation 4 and is, in the paper's own assessment, undocumented in the AMD64 Architecture Programmer's Manual. The manual specifies that loads are ordered with respect to same-cache-line stores; it does not specify ordering with respect to cache-line flushes. The empirical Zen 3 behaviour exceeds the documented contract: the silicon enforces a stronger ordering than the ISA promises. The implication for an attack relying on this behaviour is that the attack is structurally fragile — a future microarchitectural revision is permitted by the documented ISA contract to remove the stronger ordering, in which case the omitted MFENCE instructions would cease to function as expected and the activation throughput would drop. The same observation has constructive use for defensive design: an architectural assumption that is empirically observed but not formally guaranteed by the ISA contract is exactly the kind of assumption that should be either upgraded to a formal guarantee (so attackers and defenders can both rely on it) or actively broken (so attackers cannot rely on it). The current state, in which the silicon enforces what the documentation does not guarantee, is the worst case for both.

### The DDR4-to-Zen DDR5 evaluation gap

The ZenHammer DDR5 result (1 bit flip on 1 of 10 DIMMs) and the Phoenix DDR5 result (15 of 15 SK Hynix UDIMMs) are separated by less than two years of calendar time, by overlapping author sets, and by the same evaluation platform (AMD Ryzen 7 7700X). The substantive difference between the two results is the reverse-engineering layer: ZenHammer used the DDR4-derived Blacksmith pattern library directly, while Phoenix reverse-engineered the DDR5-specific TRR sampling period and constructed patterns tuned to that period. The interpretive gap is methodological rather than substrate-level. The Phoenix paper makes this explicit: the ZenHammer DDR5 result was a tooling-limited measurement, and the substrate vulnerability was always present.

The pattern illustrates a structural risk in attack research: a null result for a tool of bounded search space is not equivalent to a negative result about substrate robustness. The Blacksmith pattern library searched in a 16-tREFI window; DDR5's TRR sampling period exceeds 16 tREFI by between an order and two orders of magnitude. The probability of accidentally finding a working pattern via a DDR4-tuned fuzzer on a DDR5 substrate is approximately the probability that a DDR4-pattern accidentally aligns with a DDR5 vulnerability, which is small but non-zero. The 1-in-10 ZenHammer result is consistent with this probability; the 15-in-15 Phoenix result is consistent with the substrate being broadly vulnerable once the search space is widened.

The lesson generalises to the broader Rowhammer literature: any specific attack paper's coverage is bounded by its methodology, and absence of evidence in a specific paper does not constitute evidence of substrate-level absence of vulnerability. The Phoenix paper's research-agenda section names several gaps that its methodology does not address — Samsung and Micron DDR5, Intel CPUs, server-class RDIMMs with rank-level ECC — and explicitly characterises these as gaps in methodology rather than gaps in vulnerability. The vulnerability is presumed to extend beyond the test scope; the methodological extension required to confirm it has been deferred to subsequent work.

### The fence-policy taxonomy

The ZenHammer fence-scheduling policy taxonomy is worth recording because it generalises beyond the immediate Rowhammer context. The paper considers six policies for the placement of memory barriers (MFENCE, LFENCE, SFENCE) within a hammering pattern. SPnone places no fences at all, relying on the silicon's implicit ordering guarantees. SPBP places fences between base periods (the temporal-pattern unit from the Blacksmith notation). SPBP/2 places fences every half base period. SPpair places fences between distinct aggressor pairs. SPrep places fences between repetitions of an aggressor pair. SPfull places a fence after every access. The six policies trade ordering precision against activation throughput, with SPnone maximising throughput at the cost of ordering and SPfull maximising ordering at the cost of throughput.

The vendor-specific findings are consistent with the in-DRAM TRR characterisations from earlier work. Samsung modules are most effectively attacked under SPpair, with at least 75% of tested Samsung DIMMs producing bit flips under that policy. SK Hynix modules also respond best to SPpair across all tested DIMMs. Micron modules are unique in that SPnone produces zero flips: the lack of fences allows aggressor accesses to reorder, and Micron's TRR sampler appears to depend on command-order properties that the reordering disrupts. The vendor-dependence indicates that the fence-policy is itself a search variable in the attack space, alongside the temporal-pattern variables (Blacksmith) and the distance-structure variables (Half-Double).

### Cross-platform tooling fragility

The ZenHammer experience with the Blacksmith ref_sync routine — that it produces correct results on Intel Coffee Lake and incorrect results on AMD Zen 3, with no error indication in either case — is an instance of a more general phenomenon in low-level security tooling. Measurement tools that infer microarchitectural state from observable side effects are sensitive to the specific microarchitecture under test. A tool that works correctly on one CPU generation may produce systematically incorrect results on another generation, including a successor generation from the same vendor. The failure mode is silent: the tool does not warn, it produces wrong answers.

The Rowhammer literature exhibits this pattern at least three times. The TRRespass methodology produces correct addressing-function recovery on Intel platforms and (until DARE) incorrect recovery on AMD Zen platforms. The Blacksmith synchronisation produces correct REFRESH detection on Intel Coffee Lake and incorrect detection on AMD Zen 3 and on AMD Zen 4 with DDR5. The Blacksmith pattern library produces effective patterns on DDR4 with 16-tREFI sampling periods and ineffective patterns on DDR5 with 128-to-2608-tREFI sampling periods. In each case, the tooling assumption was implicit and the substrate variation that broke it was outside the tooling's documented assumptions. The literature's response — substrate-aware tooling, as exemplified by DARE, by the Phoenix self-correcting synchronisation, and by the Phoenix zoom-out reverse-engineering protocol — is to make the assumptions explicit and to adapt the tooling for each substrate as a research contribution in its own right.

**Assumption invalidated.** The 2016 to 2024 working assumption was that AMD Zen platforms were inherently more Rowhammer-resistant than Intel platforms, on the basis of the early Piledriver-versus-Haswell comparison and the absence of subsequent attack papers. ZenHammer falsifies this assumption: the absence of attacks reflected the absence of substrate-aware tooling, not the absence of vulnerability. With the tooling adjusted, Zen 2 and Zen 3 reproduce Intel-platform results within a factor of two on bit-flip counts and within seconds on end-to-end exploitation timing.

## §8 The Mitigation Story

The mitigation literature for Rowhammer is a sequence of architectural responses to the assumption failures documented in §§2 through 7. Reading the mitigations in order is the same as reading the attacks in order: each response addresses the most recent falsification.

### Refresh-rate doubling and Intel pTRR (2014 to 2015)

The earliest mitigations were memory-controller-side. BIOS vendors halved the refresh interval from 7.8 µs to 3.9 µs, on the principle that more frequent refreshes reduced the time available for adjacent-row disturbance to accumulate. Intel's pTRR added a per-row activation tracker to the memory controller, reading the JEDEC SPD MAC field to determine the activation threshold beyond which a row's neighbours would be preemptively refreshed. Both mitigations had performance overheads of several percent of memory bandwidth and addressed the symptom rather than the cause. The TRRespass measurement that pTRR was deployed only on a single tested server-class Intel CPU, with no consumer deployment, indicated that the memory-controller-side mitigation path had been quietly abandoned in favour of the in-DRAM path.

### In-DRAM Target Row Refresh (2016 onward)

The dominant DDR4 mitigation became the in-DRAM TRR mechanism: a sampler that tracked a small number of recently activated rows, coupled with an inhibitor that issued targeted refreshes to the neighbours of sampled rows during the existing REFRESH command's tRFC window. The mechanism was undocumented in datasheets; vendors set the SPD MAC field to "unlimited" to signal Rowhammer immunity.

The TRRespass characterisation (§3) decomposed the mechanism into its components. The sampler had a capacity of 4 to 6 rows in the studied modules; the inhibitor issued at most one or two mitigative refreshes per REFRESH command, constrained by the tRFC timing budget. Both numbers reflect silicon area trade-offs: a larger sampler would cost die area, a more aggressive inhibitor would extend tRFC and reduce effective memory bandwidth.

The mechanism failed against many-sided overflow (TRRespass), against non-uniform patterns (Blacksmith), and against the distance-2 composition (Half-Double, in which the inhibitor itself performed the attack). On every published DDR4 module evaluated post-Blacksmith, at least one pattern existed that produced bit flips with the in-DRAM TRR active. The mechanism's failure mode against Half-Double is structurally distinct from its failure modes against TRRespass and Blacksmith: the latter two overflow or evade the sampler, the former co-opts the inhibitor. A more capable sampler would address overflow and evasion but not co-option.

### ECC and ECC.fail

On-die single-bit ECC was standardised in DDR5 and is optional in earlier generations on server-class DIMMs. The mechanism corrects single-bit errors silently. Cojocar, Razavi, Giuffrida, and Bos (ECC.fail, S&P 2019) demonstrated that ECC does not prevent Rowhammer exploitation in practice for two reasons: bit flips can accumulate to a multi-bit error within an ECC codeword across refresh windows, because corrected data is not necessarily written back to the cell; and the silent correction creates a side channel that the attacker can use to identify the existence of a bit flip even when the corrected value is delivered to the requester. RowPress (§6) added a third reason: the data-dependent nature of RowPress flips and the templating requirement that they impose are compatible with ECC-correction-aware templating strategies, such as the blind-hammering approach used in Half-Double's Chromebook exploit.

### Principled academic proposals

A line of academic mitigation proposals between 2014 and 2024 attempted to provide principled, analysable defences in place of the unspecified in-DRAM mechanism. Kim et al.'s PARA (Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation), proposed in the original ISCA 2014 paper, refreshes adjacent rows with low probability on each activation; the probabilistic design makes worst-case analysis tractable. Park et al.'s Graphene (MICRO 2020) uses the Misra-Gries algorithm to track frequent rows deterministically with bounded error. Yağlıkçı et al.'s BlockHammer (HPCA 2021) throttles aggressor-row accesses by delaying activations to recently-active rows. Marazzi et al.'s ProTRR (S&P 2022) is a TRR design with formal worst-case guarantees against many-sided patterns. Saileshwar et al.'s Randomized Row-Swap (ASPLOS 2022) periodically remaps the logical-to-physical row assignment, statistically breaking the attacker's ability to maintain a fixed aggressor-victim spatial relationship.

Each of these proposals has well-characterised security properties and overheads. None of them was deployed in commodity DDR4 or DDR5 prior to 2024. The deployment gap reflects the same vendor-driven economics that produced the undocumented in-DRAM TRR: the academic proposals had public security analyses, which made them attractive to integrate, but they also had nontrivial silicon-area and bandwidth costs that vendors were reluctant to absorb in the absence of regulatory pressure.

### Refresh Management (RFM, DDR5 spec)

JEDEC's DDR5 specification introduced Refresh Management commands as an architectural mechanism for memory-controller-coordinated mitigation. The memory controller tracks per-bank activation counts and issues RFM commands when a configurable threshold is exceeded; the DRAM responds by performing a mitigation refresh during the RFM tRFC window. The mechanism is in principle a counter-based defence with public semantics. In practice, as McSee (Jattke et al., USENIX Security 2025) demonstrated, no commodity AMD Zen 4, Intel Alder Lake, or Intel Raptor Lake memory controller issues RFM commands under Rowhammer-class workloads. The mechanism is present in the specification and absent from deployed silicon, at least as of the 2025 oscilloscope traces.

### ProTRR and the principled-TRR direction

ProTRR, by Marazzi, Jattke, Solt, and Razavi at IEEE S&P 2022, is the academic proposal that occupies the principled-mitigation slot in the TRR family. The proposal accepts the in-DRAM-TRR architecture (sampler plus inhibitor) but specifies the components with formal worst-case guarantees against bounded-aggressor-count many-sided patterns. The sampler in ProTRR is sized to track every aggressor in a pattern of bounded cardinality; the inhibitor refreshes the neighbours of every tracked aggressor within a bounded number of REFRESH commands. The trade-off is silicon area: tracking more rows requires more storage, and the area cost scales linearly with the maximum supported aggressor count. The paper analyses the security-area trade-off and identifies configurations that provide TRR-compatible security at modest area overheads (a few percent of the DRAM die area).

ProTRR has not been deployed in commodity DDR4 or DDR5 silicon. The non-deployment reflects the same vendor-economic constraints that have prevented adoption of PARA, Graphene, BlockHammer, and the other academic proposals: the proposal is published with a security argument, the vendors do not adopt the proposal, and the vendor in-house mitigations continue to be undocumented. The Phoenix paper, by the same author group as ProTRR, demonstrates by experiment that the SK Hynix in-DRAM TRR mitigations of 2021 to 2024 are not implementations of ProTRR; they exhibit the structural weaknesses (limited sampler capacity, complex sampling behaviour) that ProTRR was designed to avoid.

### Randomised Row-Swap

Saileshwar et al.'s Randomized Row-Swap proposal (ASPLOS 2022) takes a different mitigation direction. Rather than tracking aggressors and refreshing victims, the proposal periodically remaps the logical-to-physical row assignment of every row in the DRAM array. The remapping breaks the spatial relationship that an attacker depends on: a victim row that is adjacent to an aggressor at time t is, after the next swap, not adjacent to the same aggressor. An attacker's templating campaign that identifies a vulnerable cell at a specific logical row is invalidated when that logical row is mapped to a different physical location. The mitigation does not prevent bit flips at the substrate level; it prevents the attacker from accumulating bit flips at specific logical addresses, which is what exploitation requires.

The Randomized Row-Swap proposal has structural advantages against Half-Double-class attacks specifically. Because the proposal does not rely on victim refresh, the TRR-co-option mechanism that Half-Double exploits is absent. The proposal is invariant to the distance structure of the attack: whether the attacker hammers at distance 1, distance 2, or distance 5, the periodic remapping disrupts the spatial relationship in the same way. The proposal also has the property that its performance overhead does not depend on the attack: every system pays the same swap cost, attacker or not, and the cost is a small percentage of memory bandwidth.

The proposal's main limitation is that it is not standardised in JEDEC and not deployed in commodity DRAM. The integration cost is non-trivial: the memory controller would need to maintain the logical-to-physical remapping table, perform the periodic swaps without disrupting normal traffic, and ensure that the swaps do not violate the JEDEC timing parameters. As with ProTRR, the proposal exists in the academic literature with security analysis and performance evaluation but has not produced vendor adoption.

### Per Row Activation Counting (PRAC, JESD79-5C, 2024)

The most structural response in the JEDEC mitigation arc is the Per Row Activation Counting standard, finalised in JEDEC JESD79-5C in July 2024. The mechanism places an activation counter on each DRAM row, inside the DRAM die itself. When a row's counter exceeds a configured threshold NBO, the DRAM raises an Alert signal on a dedicated pin. The memory controller responds to the Alert by entering an Alert Back-Off (ABO) protocol: a short window during which the controller may issue a small number of additional activations (the ABOACT window, three activations or approximately 180 nanoseconds), followed by the issuance of one to four RFM commands which the DRAM uses to mitigate the row identified as exceeding the threshold and its neighbours.

The state machine of the ABO protocol, as documented in the JEDEC specification and characterised in the QPRAC analysis, contains four states. The NORMAL state is the default condition in which the DRAM accepts activations and increments per-row counters without interfering with memory-controller traffic. A transition from NORMAL to PRE_RECOVERY occurs when any row's counter exceeds NBO; the transition is announced to the memory controller by the assertion of the Alert pin. The PRE_RECOVERY state lasts at most ABOACT in duration (approximately 180 nanoseconds), during which the memory controller may issue up to three additional activations before it must stop. A transition from PRE_RECOVERY to RECOVERY occurs at the end of the ABOACT window; in RECOVERY, the memory controller issues one to four RFM commands (the precise number being a configurable parameter Nmit), and the DRAM uses each RFM to refresh the rows with the highest activation counts in each bank. A transition from RECOVERY to DELAY occurs after the last RFM completes; the DELAY state prevents another Alert from being asserted for at least ABODelay activations, after which the state returns to NORMAL.

PRAC differs from in-DRAM TRR in three respects that matter for the security model. First, it counts rather than estimates: every activation increments the counter for the activated row, with no sampling. Second, the mechanism is public: the JEDEC specification documents the protocol, the Alert pin, and the timing parameters. Third, the action is per-row and threshold-driven rather than per-REFRESH and population-driven, which makes the worst-case behaviour analysable.

The mechanism is not yet sufficient on its own. Two structural weaknesses have been characterised in the academic literature. The first is the ABOACT window: during the up to 180 nanoseconds between the Alert assertion and the issuance of the corresponding RFM commands, the memory controller may issue activations that bypass the mitigation. An attacker who arranges to issue these activations to a specific target row can accumulate unmitigated activations on that row at a rate proportional to the Alert frequency. The Toggle+Forget attack on Panopticon (the predecessor academic design) and the Fill+Escape attack characterised by Woo et al. in the QPRAC paper (arXiv 2501.18861, 2025) both exploit this window. The second weakness is the implementation latitude that the JEDEC specification leaves to vendors: the spec requires only that the threshold NBO be less than the Rowhammer threshold TRH, without specifying the queue management policy that the DRAM uses to track which rows are candidates for mitigation when multiple rows approach the threshold simultaneously.

The Toggle+Forget attack is worth describing in detail because it illustrates the kind of structural weakness that arises from a specific PRAC implementation choice. The Panopticon design uses a small bit (the t-bit) on each row's counter to indicate when the counter has crossed a mitigation threshold. The bit toggles on each crossing; rows whose t-bits have toggled are inserted into a service FIFO that the mitigation logic drains. The attack proceeds by first activating each of Q+1 rows uniformly to (M-1) activations, where Q is the FIFO depth and M is the mitigation threshold. The attacker then activates the first Q rows once each, causing their t-bits to toggle and their counters to be inserted into the FIFO; the FIFO fills, and the next t-bit toggle (on the Q+1th row) cannot be inserted. During the ABOACT window that follows the Alert, the attacker activates the target row (the Q+1th) twice; the activations are not mitigated because the row is not in the FIFO. The t-bit on the target row will not toggle again until 2^t additional activations have occurred, by which time many additional unmitigated activations have accumulated. The Fill+Escape attack is structurally similar but does not require the t-bit assumption: it relies only on the FIFO being full when the target row's count crosses M, with the FIFO drained at the end of the RECOVERY state and refilled by additional decoy activations before the next Alert.

The structural weakness exhibited by Panopticon and similar FIFO-based PRAC implementations is that the queue, when full, cannot admit new candidates regardless of how high their activation count rises. An attacker who keeps the queue full of decoys can keep the target row out of the queue indefinitely. The QPRAC priority-queue design closes this weakness by making the queue intentionally full at all times and replacing the lowest-count entry with any new candidate that exceeds it. Under this design, no decoy strategy can keep the target row out of the queue once its count exceeds the lowest count in the queue. The structural guarantee — that the row with the highest activation count is always the head of the queue — is what permits the formal security bound that the QPRAC paper proves.

### QPRAC

The QPRAC proposal (Woo et al., arXiv 2501.18861, 2025) addresses both weaknesses with a Priority-Based Service Queue (PSQ): a per-bank queue of fixed depth that holds rows in descending activation-count order, with the highest-count row at the head. When a new row's activation count exceeds the lowest-count row in the queue, the new row replaces the old one. When the head's count exceeds NBO, the Alert is raised. When RFM commands arrive, they target the head of the queue, then the next-highest, and so on. The priority-ordered structure resists Fill+Escape because an attacker attempting to refill the queue with low-count decoys cannot prevent the target row from rising to the head once its activation count is high enough.

The QPRAC paper provides a formal security bound: the design is secure if TRH > NBO + Nonline, where Nonline is the maximum number of activations that can bypass mitigation through the ABOACT window plus the ABODelay plus the queue's blast radius. For a five-entry queue with PRAC-1 (single RFM per Alert), Nonline is bounded at 46, giving a minimum secure TRH of NBO + 46. The bound is tight in the sense that the paper exhibits attack constructions that reach the bound.

The QPRAC opportunistic mitigation extension uses the all-bank RFM that DDR5 issues when an Alert is raised: while one bank is being mitigated, the other 31 banks each refresh the head of their respective PSQs. The proactive mitigation extension refreshes the PSQ head during every REFRESH whose head exceeds NPRO = NBO / 2, with energy overhead 1.9% over a non-proactive baseline.

The QPRAC formal security bound deserves separate consideration because it represents the first machine-checked security argument in the deployed-mitigation tier of the Rowhammer literature. The argument has three parts. The first part bounds the maximum number of activations that can bypass mitigation between successive Alert-RFM cycles; this number, Nonline, is the sum of the ABOACT window contribution (3 activations), the ABODelay contribution (Nmit activations, where Nmit is 1, 2, or 4 depending on configuration), and the blast-radius contribution (2 activations on neighbouring rows that the mitigation does not directly cover). The second part bounds the pool of rows that can accumulate activations between Alert cycles; this bound is a function of the queue depth and the rate at which rows are evicted from the queue. The third part connects the two bounds to a security condition of the form TRH > NBO + Nonline. Under PRAC-1 (single RFM per Alert), the bound is Nonline ≤ 46 for a five-entry PSQ; the implied minimum TRH for security is NBO + 46. At NBO = 32 (a representative configuration), the bound is TRH > 71. The bound is tight in the sense that the paper exhibits attack constructions that approach but do not exceed it. The bound is also conservative in the sense that it accounts for transitive mitigation effects — when a mitigation refresh of one row also refreshes that row's neighbours, those neighbours' counters are reset and contribute to the bound's tightness.

The HammerWatch proposal (Herrmann et al., arXiv 2603.24172, 2026) extends the PRAC mitigation arc in a different direction: rather than improving the prevention guarantee, it adds an attestation mechanism that allows a remote verifier to detect that Rowhammer-class activity has occurred on a target system. The mechanism uses two measurement sources. The first is the Linux EDAC (Error Detection and Correction) subsystem, which collects machine-check exceptions from the memory controller when ECC errors exceed correction capacity; the second is the Alert pin from PRAC, which the memory controller can report to the operating system when assertions occur. The two measurement streams are accumulated into a TPM PCR via a hash-chain extension protocol; the PCR value is signed by the TPM private key and returned to the verifier on demand. The detection threshold is configured at three or more Alert events plus three or more uncorrectable ECC events within a configurable window. The proposal's structural significance is the shift from prevention to detection: where PRAC and QPRAC attempt to make Rowhammer attacks structurally impossible, HammerWatch accepts that the prevention may fail and adds an after-the-fact detection mechanism that allows infrastructure to isolate compromised systems. The two approaches are complementary rather than competing.

### The mitigation arc as a whole

Reading the mitigations chronologically yields a clear arc: refresh doubling → in-DRAM TRR → ECC → academic proposals → RFM → PRAC → QPRAC. Each step responds to a falsified assumption of the previous step. Refresh doubling assumed that timing alone was sufficient; TRR added per-row tracking. TRR assumed obscurity was sufficient; TRRespass and Blacksmith broke it. ECC assumed silent correction was sufficient; ECC.fail and the cumulative-error mode broke it. RFM moved the tracking to the memory controller, in principle; deployment data shows it has not been activated. PRAC moved the counting back into the DRAM with a public specification; the ABOACT window and the queue-management latitude remained as residual weaknesses. QPRAC closes the queue-management weakness with a formal bound; the ABOACT window remains an architectural limit of the JEDEC PRAC protocol that no purely-DRAM-side mitigation can fully eliminate.

The relationship between academic mitigation proposals and JEDEC standards is itself worth observing. The academic proposals — PARA, Graphene, BlockHammer, ProTRR, Randomized Row-Swap, Panopticon — preceded the JEDEC PRAC specification by between one and ten years. Each proposal was published with a security analysis and a performance evaluation in academic conferences, with the source code typically released. None was adopted in deployed commodity DRAM prior to the JEDEC standardisation of PRAC in 2024. The deployment gap reflects the asymmetry between research and standardisation: a research proposal becomes a JEDEC standard only when the major DRAM vendors agree on a common specification, and the major vendors have incentives to delay specification work that would require revealing implementation details of competing in-house mitigations. The PRAC specification is, in this reading, the negotiated outcome of vendor consensus on what a public counter-based mitigation should look like; the specific design choices reflect implementation constraints that the vendors share rather than the optimum that the academic literature has identified.

The performance cost of PRAC, as a function of threshold parameters, is itself a security-relevant axis. At NBO = 128 (a conservative threshold corresponding to TRH = 1280 or higher), the multi-bank-simultaneous-hammering performance attack characterised in the QPRAC paper reduces system memory bandwidth by 62%. At NBO = 64, the reduction is approximately 80%. At NBO = 32 (a more security-aggressive setting), the reduction is approximately 90%. At NBO = 16, the reduction is 93%. The reduction reflects the fact that a sustained pattern of activations on each of the 32 banks can keep all 32 banks in the all-bank-RFM state much of the time, stalling the controller. The mitigation against the performance attack — switching from all-bank RFM to per-bank or same-bank RFM (RFMsb or RFMpb) — requires changes to the JEDEC DDR5 specification that have not yet been ratified. The trade-off between security (low NBO) and performance (high NBO) is therefore not a free parameter; vendors choosing aggressive NBO values expose their systems to a performance-availability attack, and vendors choosing conservative NBO values expose their systems to a confidentiality-integrity attack. The current JEDEC specification permits either choice without forcing the trade-off.

The arc is incomplete. RowPress (§6) attacks a dimension that none of the deployed counter-based defences track. The DDR5 specification does not require row-open duration tracking, and the DDR6 specification, projected for 2027 or later, will be the first JEDEC standard to formally consider it. Half-Double-class attacks against PRAC have not been demonstrated in the public literature, but the structural argument — that mitigative refreshes themselves perform near-distance activations — applies in principle to any PRAC implementation whose mitigation step activates neighbouring rows. The defence-in-depth picture that PRAC, ProTRR, and Randomized Row-Swap together imply is the direction the literature has converged on; deployment of that picture in commodity silicon remains future work.

### The cumulative cost picture

The cumulative cost of the Rowhammer mitigation arc, viewed across a decade, is non-trivial. The refresh-doubling stopgap imposed a 5-to-10% memory bandwidth tax across the entire DDR3 and early-DDR4 deployment base. The in-DRAM TRR mitigation occupies silicon area on every DDR4 and DDR5 chip; the area cost is not in the public domain but is estimated by academic analysis to be on the order of single-digit percent of the chip's logic area. The on-die ECC mechanism on DDR5 imposes a 12% parity overhead on every DDR5 codeword, which translates to a few-percent memory-bandwidth and capacity cost. The PRAC mechanism in JESD79-5C imposes additional silicon area for the per-row counters (estimated at 1-2% of die area, depending on counter width) and adds the Alert pin to the DRAM-package interface. The QPRAC priority queue adds a small CAM per bank, with the cost reported in the QPRAC paper at approximately 15 bytes per bank.

Aggregated, the mitigation cost across a single DDR5 DIMM is on the order of single-digit percent of die area plus single-digit percent of bandwidth plus the parity overhead of ECC. The cost is shared across all DRAM customers regardless of whether they face the Rowhammer threat model. Commodity desktop users, who in practice do not face the cross-tenant exploitation scenario that motivates much of the literature, pay the same mitigation cost as cloud providers running mixed-tenant workloads. The mitigation cost is therefore a shared externality, paid by the entire DRAM customer base on behalf of the subset for whom the threat model is realistic.

The shared-externality structure influences vendor incentives in ways that the literature has observed indirectly. Vendor adoption of more expensive mitigations (ProTRR, Randomized Row-Swap) is gated by whether the increment in security justifies the increment in cost across the entire customer base. The negotiated outcome — JEDEC PRAC with vendor-specific implementation latitude — provides a baseline that all vendors can ship at acceptable area cost, while leaving the more aggressive mitigations to specific products targeting the cloud customer segment. The structure permits per-product mitigation differentiation, but the public literature does not yet document whether vendors are exploiting this option in deployed products.

**Assumption invalidated.** The mitigation literature has falsified, in sequence, the assumptions that refresh timing alone is sufficient (refresh doubling), that obscurity is sufficient (in-DRAM TRR), that single-bit correction is sufficient (ECC), that memory-controller-side counters are sufficient if specified (RFM in absence of deployment), and that public per-row counters are sufficient on their own (PRAC against Toggle+Forget and Fill+Escape). The current frontier — PRAC with a priority-ordered queue, formal worst-case bounds, and protection against row-open-duration attacks — is itself a hypothesis that subsequent work will test.

## §8.5 The Obscurity Pattern

A cross-cutting theme of the Rowhammer literature, visible across §§2 through 8, is the role of obscurity in the vendor-side response to academic findings. The pattern recurs with sufficient regularity to merit isolation as a topic in its own right.

The TRRespass disclosure (2020) revealed that the in-DRAM TRR mechanism, which DRAM vendors had been shipping since approximately 2016 and which their marketing represented as Rowhammer-immune, was an undocumented streaming top-k estimator with bounded sampler capacity. The mechanism was protected by absence from datasheets; vendors disclosed no technical details about its operation, no parameters that would permit security analysis, and no claims that were verifiable by independent measurement. The TRRespass discovery cost was approximately two person-years of FPGA-based reverse engineering; the bypass cost, given the published TRRespass methodology, was hours per module. The protection that obscurity provided was therefore an asymmetric one: high discovery cost, low replication cost once discovery was complete.

The Half-Double disclosure (2021-2022) revealed that the distance-2 effect was a known phenomenon among DRAM vendors, with the mechanism (TRR co-option) understood internally and protected by contractual confidentiality among industry participants. The fifteen-month embargo period that the academic paper observed reflected the structure of the disclosure: Google security engineers, AWS engineers, and other industry-affiliated co-authors were under separate vendor NDAs that limited what could be said about specific vendor implementations. The paper's anonymisation of vendor identities for the commercial SoC platform (Section 5.2) was a direct consequence of these NDAs.

The Blacksmith disclosure (2021-2022) produced the most direct quotation about the disclosure-and-mitigation cycle: "None of the contacted parties informed us of their mitigation plans." Nine months elapsed between the Q2 2021 vendor notification and the Q4 2021 public disclosure, during which the vendors received the technical details and were given the opportunity to respond. The response, as documented in the paper, was silence: SK Hynix engaged in private discussion of the LPDDR4X results, Samsung and Micron did not produce public statements, Intel discussed mitigation possibilities privately, and the public-facing vendor record contained no acknowledgment of the work.

The RowPress disclosure (2023) added an additional layer of obscurity to the picture. The paper's footnote 1 documented that Micron had filed defensive patents on the underlying physical effect ("RAS Clobber") in 2014 and 2018, predating the academic discovery by nearly a decade. Samsung's contemporaneous arXiv preprint used the marketing-safe "passing gate effect" naming for the same phenomenon, disclosed concurrently with the RowPress academic paper but framed as a routine device-physics observation rather than as a security-relevant attack vector. The decade-long gap between Micron's 2014 patent and the 2023 academic disclosure represents the most extreme case of the obscurity pattern: a security-relevant physical phenomenon was documented in defensive intellectual property nearly a decade before the academic literature characterised it.

The Phoenix disclosure (2025-2026) repeated the pattern in the DDR5 era. AMD issued a BIOS update on 12 September 2025 in response to the disclosure; the update switches the memory controller to Fine-Granularity Refresh (FGR) mode. The Phoenix authors were unable to verify independently that the FGR mode mitigates the attack — the paper notes that FGR increases refresh frequency while reducing per-REFRESH time, with the net effect on mitigation unclear. The vendor response was a deployed mitigation whose effectiveness was not characterised in the public literature at the time of the disclosure; the asymmetry between vendor and academic information continued.

The cumulative pattern across these five disclosures is that vendors prefer obscurity over disclosure when the choice is available. The reasons are economic and strategic: undocumented mitigations have negotiating leverage in industry standards bodies, defensive patents have value in cross-licensing arrangements, and silence avoids public statements that could attract regulatory attention. The cumulative effect on the literature is that the academic record is the primary source of public information about deployed Rowhammer mitigations, with the vendor side of the story available only through measurement and inference. The two sides do not engage on equal terms: the academic side discloses methodology and data openly, while the vendor side withholds implementation details and deployment timelines.

The deployment of the JEDEC PRAC specification in 2024 represents a partial departure from the obscurity pattern. The specification is public, the protocol is documented, and the security analysis (in QPRAC and related papers) is conducted on the basis of the published specification. The departure is partial because the specification leaves significant implementation latitude to vendors: the queue management policy, the precise threshold values, and the mitigation strategies during the ABOACT window are not specified. The vendor-specific implementations remain undocumented and may, in time, recapitulate the obscurity pattern that the in-DRAM TRR mechanism exhibited a decade earlier.

The structural implication of the obscurity pattern for the field's future is that vendor disclosure norms are unlikely to change without external pressure. The academic literature has not, to date, produced effective leverage for changing the norms: papers describe the pattern, propose mitigations, and document the asymmetry, but the vendor-side response remains discretionary. Regulatory frameworks that require disclosure of hardware vulnerabilities — analogous to the software vulnerability disclosure frameworks that have developed in the past two decades — would change the incentive structure but do not currently exist for hardware security. The Cyber Resilience Act in the European Union, and similar regulatory initiatives elsewhere, may extend mandatory disclosure to hardware vulnerabilities in coming years; the implications for the Rowhammer literature and for vendor practice are not yet visible.

**Assumption invalidated.** The cumulative assumption of the vendor side of the Rowhammer story, across a decade of academic disclosure, is that obscurity provides security. The academic record falsifies this assumption: every undocumented mitigation has been reverse-engineered and bypassed within months of becoming the dominant defence, every defensive patent has been ignored as a relevance signal, and every period of vendor silence has been followed by an academic paper that confirms what the silence concealed. The obscurity pattern persists not because it provides security but because it preserves vendor optionality in the absence of regulatory pressure to disclose.

### Cross-paper continuity

The Rowhammer literature exhibits a degree of cross-paper continuity that is uncommon in security research. Many of the papers are produced by overlapping author sets at a small number of research groups: COMSEC at ETH Zurich (TRRespass, Blacksmith, ZenHammer, Phoenix, ProTRR, McSee), SAFARI at ETH and CMU (RowPress, BlockHammer, the various U-TRR descendants, the wordline-voltage characterisation), Graz TU (Half-Double, Rowhammer.js, the sudo binary attack), VUSec at VU Amsterdam (TRRespass, Drammer, SMASH, Flip Feng Shui). The continuity reflects the technical depth required: a research group that has invested in FPGA-based DRAM tooling, in commodity-platform memory-controller reverse engineering, and in DRAM substrate characterisation is positioned to extend its work across multiple papers, while a group lacking the tooling base must reproduce it before any new contribution is possible.

The continuity has a downside that the literature has not extensively addressed. The same research groups that produce attack papers also produce mitigation papers; the same groups that conduct vendor-disclosed coordinated disclosure also publish the academic record of the disclosure. The information flow between vendors and the academic literature is mediated by a small set of researchers under varying NDAs. The published record is the public-domain projection of a richer private conversation; the assumptions about what vendors know, when they knew it, and how they responded are inferences drawn from the public record rather than statements derived from public disclosure. The literature has, in places (Half-Double, Phoenix), been explicit about NDAs limiting what can be disclosed; the broader pattern of vendor-academic information asymmetry is structural and has not been addressed by changes in disclosure practice.

### Reproducibility track record

The reproducibility of Rowhammer papers in the public literature has improved substantially over the decade. The 2014 Kim et al. paper provided high-level methodology but not source code; reproductions required reimplementation. The 2020 TRRespass paper provided source code and documentation; reproductions on the released test set are routine. The 2022 Blacksmith paper provided source code, the trained pattern parameters from the 24-hour calibration run, and the DRAMA-derived addressing functions for the test platform; independent reproduction (Gruss group at ESORICS 2023) confirmed the bit-flip generation on the released DIMMs. The 2024 ZenHammer paper provided source code and the DARE addressing-function recovery tool. The 2026 Phoenix paper, at the time of writing, has the source code, the FPGA bug fixes (documented in Appendix A of the paper), and the self-correcting synchronisation routine available for download.

The reproducibility trajectory is one of the methodological successes of the field. The combination of academic-conference open-artifact requirements, the institutional support from the participating research groups, and the precedent set by early papers like TRRespass has produced an expectation that Rowhammer papers will release executable code and characterisation data. The expectation has not extended to vendor-side empirical data — DRAM vendors have not, in general, released characterisation data for their own products — but the academic side of the literature is now comparable in reproducibility to the software-security side that originated the open-artifact movement.

**Assumption invalidated.** The implicit assumption of any single paper is that its sample is representative of its target population. The literature as a whole shows that the population is heterogeneous along multiple axes — vendor, die revision, production date, density, manufacturing process — and that bit-flip susceptibility varies on each. A claim that a paper's methodology "breaks DDR4" is most precise when read as a claim that the methodology has produced bit flips on a specified set of test modules under specified conditions. The next module from the same vendor, manufactured six months later, may require methodology adjustments before producing flips at all.

### The substrate scaling trajectory

The HCmin trajectory across generations is one of the clearest quantitative trends in the Rowhammer literature. DDR3 at the 30-to-40 nm node measured 139,000 activations; DDR4 at the 20-to-25 nm node measured 45,000; DDR5 at the 14-to-18 nm node measured approximately 32,000. The trajectory implies a power-law scaling with node feature size, consistent with the physical interpretation that smaller cells store less charge and are positioned closer to neighbouring wordlines. Extrapolation along the same trajectory places projected DDR6 HCmin (at the 8-to-12 nm node, projected for mid-decade) below 20,000 activations.

The implication for the mitigation arc is that the activation budget available to an attacker, expressed as the ratio of the maximum activations per refresh window to the HCmin, has been growing across generations. At a maximum of approximately 1.4 million activations per 64-millisecond DDR4 refresh window and an HCmin of 45,000, the budget ratio is approximately 31. At a maximum of approximately 2.0 million activations per 32-millisecond DDR5 refresh window and an HCmin of 32,000, the budget ratio is approximately 62. The attack difficulty, expressed as the inverse of the budget ratio, has decreased by approximately a factor of two between DDR4 and DDR5. Subsequent generations are projected to continue this trajectory absent substrate-level redesign.

The mitigation requirements scale correspondingly. A mitigation that is just sufficient at DDR4 (intercepting attacks at activation counts above the substrate HCmin) is insufficient at DDR5 (where the same activation-count threshold corresponds to a larger ratio above HCmin). Per Row Activation Counting addresses this by counting every activation and refreshing at NBO below HCmin; the design is intrinsically scaling-aware, in that the same protocol works at any HCmin so long as NBO is set appropriately. The trade-off is that the silicon area required for the per-row counters scales with the number of rows per bank, and the row count per bank has been growing with each generation. The area-vs-security trade-off is fundamental to PRAC-class mitigations and is the subject of ongoing optimisation work.

### Cross-cutting empirical patterns

Several empirical patterns recur across the studies and are worth consolidating.

The first is that bit-flip count per templating campaign varies by approximately four orders of magnitude across modules within a single vendor's product line and approximately five orders of magnitude across vendors. The variance is consistent with the technology-scaling explanation (newer parts more vulnerable) combined with process-variation effects (within-batch variability is comparable to within-vendor variability). The implication for exploitation is that templating time is itself variable: a few seconds on the most vulnerable modules to many minutes or hours on the least vulnerable.

The second is that bit-flip directionality is consistent within a class of read disturbance but inverted between classes. Classical Rowhammer is dominated by 0-to-1 flips in true-cell layouts; RowPress is dominated by 1-to-0 flips. The directionality reflects the underlying physical mechanism: charge injection for Rowhammer, charge extraction for RowPress. Mitigation strategies that monitor parity (a single bit indicating whether a row has changed) are equally effective against both directions; strategies that monitor specific bits (e.g., the high bits of a Physical Frame Number) may be effective against one direction and not the other.

The third is that temperature dependence is consistent across vendors within each phenomenon class. Classical Rowhammer exhibits a non-monotonic temperature response with moderate sensitivity; RowPress exhibits a monotonic temperature response with strong sensitivity. The temperature regime relevant for production deployment (50 to 80°C chip temperature) is precisely the regime in which RowPress sensitivity is amplified relative to laboratory characterisations conducted at 50°C.

The fourth is that the exploitation surface — the classes of attack that can be constructed from a Rowhammer primitive — has remained essentially constant across the decade. The three classical exploits (PTE attack, RSA-2048 key corruption, sudo binary attack) characterised in 2015-2018 work continue to be the standard exploitation targets in the 2024-2026 papers. The exploit framework is decoupled from the bit-flip-triggering layer: improvements in the triggering layer translate directly to improvements in exploitation speed and reliability, without requiring new exploit constructions.

The fifth is that the disclosure-to-deployment latency for mitigations is on the order of several years. TRRespass disclosed in 2020; the JEDEC response (PRAC) was standardised in 2024. The four-year gap reflects the time required for vendor consensus on a standard plus the additional time for vendor production of compliant silicon. The implication is that mitigation deployment lags attack disclosure by enough that any specific generation of DRAM is vulnerable for its entire deployment lifetime once an attack is published. DDR4 modules manufactured in 2018, with in-DRAM TRR as the only mitigation, will be Rowhammer-vulnerable for the rest of their deployed lifetime regardless of subsequent mitigation development.

## §10 Open Questions

The Rowhammer literature in 2026 has, by the standards of the field, addressed most of the questions that the 2014 paper opened. The questions that remain open in 2026 are of three kinds: physical questions about cell-level mechanisms, architectural questions about mitigation design under multi-axis disturbance, and methodological questions about how the experimental tooling generalises to new substrates.

**1. Cell-level model of RowPress and the relationship to RowHammer.** The phenomenological evidence for distinct cell populations between RowHammer and RowPress (overlap below 0.013%) and for inverted flip directionality is strong. The microscale physical model — what specifically passes through the gate of a victim cell's transistor when the adjacent wordline is held high — is not yet settled in the public literature. Samsung's "passing gate effect" arXiv preprint and the Micron RAS Clobber patents describe phenomenology; a circuit-level simulation that reproduces the observed sensitivities across vendors and die revisions has not been published. Without it, the cross-vendor universality of the slope coefficients (−1.00 to −1.02 in log-log) is observed but not predicted.

**2. PRAC under Half-Double-class compositional attacks.** The structural argument that mitigative refreshes themselves perform near-distance activations applies to any PRAC implementation whose RFM-driven mitigation step activates neighbouring rows. The argument has not been empirically demonstrated against a deployed PRAC implementation, because deployed PRAC implementations are not yet in widespread commodity silicon. When they are, the question of whether a Half-Double analogue exists for PRAC will become testable. The pre-emptive design response — that PRAC mitigation should refresh victim rows in a way that does not itself constitute activation, possibly via a separate sense-amplifier-based recovery rather than a full ACT — has been proposed in academic discussion but not specified by JEDEC.

**3. Wordline-voltage and other axes of read disturbance.** Yağlıkçı et al. (MICRO 2024) characterised the effect of reduced wordline voltage on both RowHammer and RowPress, finding that lower voltage reduces both. The result implies a third axis — voltage — in addition to access count and row-open duration. Whether further axes exist (refresh-timing patterns, command-bus traffic, temperature schedules) is an open empirical question. A systematic methodology for axis discovery has not been published; the existing axes were discovered ad hoc.

**4. Composition of Rowhammer with other microarchitectural attacks.** Half-Double's use of Spectre as a crash-safe oracle is one demonstrated composition. PRAC's Alert pin is a side channel that can be observed from privileged contexts and possibly inferred from unprivileged contexts via timing; the consequences of such inference for confidentiality (whether the Alert pattern leaks information about a co-tenant's memory behaviour) is unexplored. Compositions with transient-execution attacks, with cache side channels, and with bus-level information leakage have not been systematically surveyed.

**5. Reverse-engineering scaling for new vendors and generations.** The Phoenix authors estimate a half person-year of effort per additional vendor for DDR5 TRR characterisation. The literature does not yet contain methodology for partially automating this effort. Grey-box approaches that combine black-box fuzzing with limited reverse engineering — the post-Blacksmith / U-TRR direction — improve the constant but do not reduce the asymptotic effort. A general framework for inferring a streaming-counter-based mitigation's parameters from black-box bit-flip observations would change the cost structure of the attack-defence asymmetry.

**6. Server-class memory and rank-level ECC.** The published end-to-end Rowhammer exploits operate on consumer and Chromebook-class hardware. Server-class memory with rank-level ECC, RDIMMs with chipkill, and LRDIMMs with buffered registers are absent or sparsely represented in the public literature. The substrate physics is the same; the mitigation stack is different. The question of whether the academic methodology generalises to the server-class memory stack at present rests on private vendor evaluations that are not in the public domain.

**7. Capability-based and formal-verification approaches to Rowhammer-class faults.** Memory capability systems — CHERI, seL4 with extended capabilities — operate at abstraction levels above the DRAM substrate. The ZenHammer entry in this writeup's source vault notes that capability-based mitigation against Rowhammer-class faults would require lifting substrate properties (DRAM bank and row provenance) into the capability type system. No deployed capability system currently does this. Whether such lifting is feasible, and what its overhead would be, is an open architectural question. The QPRAC formal bound is itself a step in this direction: it demonstrates that mitigations at the DRAM-mechanism level admit machine-checkable security arguments, but the arguments rest on assumed substrate properties (TRH, NBO) that are not themselves formally derived.

The Siloz proposal (Loughlin et al., SOSP 2023) takes a step in this direction at the operating-system level. Siloz introduces DRAM isolation domains in the OS memory allocator: physical pages allocated to different security domains are placed in different DRAM banks, with the per-bank Rowhammer threshold serving as the isolation boundary. The proposal prevents cross-domain Rowhammer attacks by construction, with overhead determined by the allocator's ability to satisfy bank-level placement constraints. The proposal does not prevent intra-domain attacks, and it requires the OS to know the DRAM bank mapping function. The Siloz direction generalises naturally to capability-based systems, where the substrate-aware capability would carry the bank identifier and the kernel would enforce per-bank-isolation invariants on capability propagation.

**8. The interaction between Rowhammer mitigations and confidential computing.** Confidential computing technologies (AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, ARM CCA) provide memory encryption and integrity protection between a tenant and the hypervisor. The integrity protection prevents the hypervisor from observing or modifying a tenant's data; it does not prevent a tenant from inducing bit flips in the tenant's own memory via Rowhammer, nor does it prevent cross-tenant Rowhammer where two tenants share the same DRAM banks. The interaction between PRAC-class mitigations and confidential-computing memory layouts is unexplored in the public literature. Whether PRAC Alert events are visible across the confidential-computing boundary, whether the Alert pin can be used as a side channel by a co-tenant, and whether the all-bank RFM stalls under multi-bank hammering create cross-tenant performance interference are open questions.

**9. Browser-based and JavaScript-based Rowhammer in the PRAC era.** Rowhammer.js (Gruss et al., DIMVA 2016) and SMASH (de Ridder et al., USENIX Security 2021) demonstrated Rowhammer from JavaScript executing in a browser, removing the requirement for native code execution. Posthammer (de Ridder et al., USENIX Security 2025) extended the result with refresh-injection techniques. The browser-context attacks have not been demonstrated against PRAC-protected DDR5; the timing resolution available to JavaScript (limited by browser sandboxing) may not support the alignment precision that the Phoenix-class patterns require. Whether browser-context Rowhammer can be made to work against PRAC, and what specific browser mitigations (timer-resolution reduction, SharedArrayBuffer restriction) are effective against it, is an ongoing research question.

**10. The DDR6 and LPDDR6 specification process.** The DDR6 JEDEC specification, projected for ratification in 2027 or later, will be the first specification developed entirely in the post-Rowhammer era. The pre-publication discussion has, on the basis of public statements from JEDEC working-group members, considered making PRAC mandatory rather than optional, adding row-open duration tracking, and tightening the ABOACT window. The specific provisions of the eventual specification will be a major determinant of the Rowhammer landscape in the 2030s. The LPDDR6 specification, for mobile and embedded applications, faces a different trade-off: the area and power costs of PRAC-class mitigations matter more in the mobile context, and the LPDDR product line has historically been more vulnerable than the desktop product line.

### A note on what is not yet known

The Rowhammer literature has, after twelve years of intensive work, characterised the substrate-level mechanism, mapped the temporal and spatial attack-pattern axes, and produced both attack and mitigation proposals that exhibit a recognisable structure. Several questions remain open even at the level of basic phenomenology.

The cell-level physics of charge migration under Rowhammer and RowPress conditions is not fully modelled in the public literature. The Samsung "passing gate effect" arXiv preprint and the Walker et al. IEEE TED 2021 paper sketch device-physics interpretations, but a complete circuit-level simulation that reproduces the observed scaling, temperature, and data-pattern dependences across vendors is not available. The cumulative effect of repeated activation on the cell's retention characteristics across refresh windows — whether a cell that has experienced Rowhammer stress becomes permanently more susceptible — has not been characterised in long-term studies.

The interaction between Rowhammer and DRAM refresh management at the system level is partially understood. Memory controllers can postpone refresh by up to nine intervals under the JEDEC specification; this postponement is opportunistic, performance-driven, and largely transparent to the operating system. An attacker who induces a refresh-postponement pattern (by directing memory traffic in ways that the controller responds to with postponement) gains tAggON extension, which contributes to RowPress effectiveness. The precise control loops involved have not been documented for any commodity memory controller; the public characterisations have inferred behaviour from observation rather than from vendor disclosure.

The relationship between Rowhammer and other read-disturbance phenomena (whether documented in the academic literature or referenced only in vendor patents) is not fully mapped. The Micron RAS Clobber patents describe phenomenology that overlaps with RowPress; whether they describe additional phenomena that have not yet appeared in academic characterisation is not currently known publicly. Samsung's defensive patents on related phenomena (wordline-coupling effects, sense-amplifier asymmetries) are similarly opaque to academic readers. The vendor patent literature is a parallel record of the physical phenomenology that is less accessible than the academic record but that may contain anticipations of phenomena the academic literature has not yet identified.

## Appendix on the Industry Standards Process

The JEDEC Solid State Technology Association is the standards body responsible for the DDR3, DDR4, DDR5, and LPDDR specifications. The JEDEC standards process is conducted in working groups that include representatives from the major DRAM vendors (Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron, and others), the major memory-controller vendors (Intel, AMD, NVIDIA, ARM, and others), and the major system integrators (cloud providers, server OEMs). The working group meetings are not public; the resulting standards are published but are available only to JEDEC members, with the public-facing version typically delayed by several months.

The Rowhammer-relevant JEDEC actions are recorded in the public standards documents. JESD79-4 (DDR4) added the SPD MAC field in its initial release; the MAC encoding was specified, and the field was made available to memory-controller designers who chose to implement pTRR. JESD79-5 (DDR5, 2020) introduced the RFM command and the on-die ECC mechanism. JESD79-5A through JESD79-5B (2021-2023) refined the DDR5 specification with implementation details. JESD79-5C (July 2024) added the Per Row Activation Counting mechanism and the Alert pin. Each amendment reflects the negotiated outcome of working-group discussion; the rationale for specific design choices is not always documented in the public-facing record.

The relationship between the academic literature and the JEDEC standards process is mediated by a small number of researchers who participate in both. The participation is not always disclosed in the academic papers; some authors of academic papers are also JEDEC working-group members, and their academic publications may reflect the working-group discussions in ways that the publication itself does not document. The pattern is structural rather than discrete: the academic community and the JEDEC standards community share members, sources, and discussions, but the participation is not formalised. The implication is that the JEDEC standards reflect academic findings with some lag and some filtering; the academic literature reflects JEDEC standards work with similar lag and filtering. The two records are not independent.

The PRAC specification in JESD79-5C is the clearest case of the academic literature directly influencing a JEDEC standard. The specification's NBO threshold, the ABO protocol, the Alert pin mechanism, and the RFM-based mitigation step are all visible in the academic literature on Panopticon and predecessor proposals. The specification's design constraints — silicon area, timing budget, command-bus compatibility — are clearly informed by JEDEC working-group considerations rather than by academic preference alone. The result is a specification that is recognisably academic in inspiration but JEDEC in execution, with the academic and standards-process inputs visible in different parts of the design.

## Appendix on the Architectural Question of Where the Mitigation Should Live

A recurring architectural question across the mitigation arc is which component of the system should be responsible for Rowhammer mitigation. Four candidates have appeared in the literature.

The first is the operating system. OS-level mitigations include the Siloz proposal (bank-level isolation of security domains), kernel-side allocator hardening (placing sensitive data on physical addresses that are unlikely to be Rowhammered), and ANVIL's hardware-performance-counter-based detection. The advantage of OS-level mitigations is that they can be deployed via software updates without requiring hardware changes. The disadvantage is that the OS lacks visibility into the DRAM substrate; the OS knows the page address it has allocated but does not know the DRAM bank and row in which the page is physically located unless the OS has been extended with DRAM-mapping awareness.

The second is the memory controller. MC-level mitigations include Intel pTRR (counter-based mitigation at the controller), RFM (controller-issued mitigation commands to the DRAM), and various academic proposals (PARA, Graphene, BlockHammer). The advantage of MC-level mitigations is that they have full visibility into the activation pattern that the controller issues to the DRAM. The disadvantage is that they require coordination between the controller manufacturer and the DRAM manufacturer; mitigations at the controller side only work if the DRAM side supports them via RFM-like commands.

The third is the DRAM itself. DRAM-level mitigations include in-DRAM TRR, ProTRR, Randomized Row-Swap, Panopticon, PRAC, and QPRAC. The advantage of DRAM-level mitigations is that they have full visibility into the DRAM substrate and can be triggered by purely DRAM-internal events without controller cooperation. The disadvantage is that DRAM silicon is expensive in area cost; per-row state requires storage that scales with the number of rows.

The fourth is the interface between the DRAM and the controller — the command bus and the optional pins (Alert, RFM). PRAC sits at this interface: the per-row counters are in the DRAM, the Alert is a DRAM-to-controller signal, and the RFM response is a controller-to-DRAM command. The advantage of interface-level mitigation is that it decomposes the mitigation responsibility: the DRAM knows when to trigger, the controller knows how to respond. The disadvantage is that the interface protocol must be standardised across all DRAM and controller vendors, which has historically been slow (the four-year gap from TRRespass to PRAC reflects this).

The current consensus, embodied in JEDEC JESD79-5C, is that interface-level mitigation with the responsibility split between DRAM (counting) and controller (responding) is the right architecture. The split places the counting work at the side that has the visibility (DRAM) and the response work at the side that has the scheduling flexibility (controller). The remaining design questions — queue management in the DRAM, RFM scheduling in the controller — are within each side's responsibility. The architectural decision constrains the implementation space without prescribing a specific implementation.

## Appendix on Research Groups

A small number of research groups have produced the majority of the public Rowhammer literature. Naming them and briefly characterising their contributions provides context for the citation lattice.

**COMSEC at ETH Zurich**, led by Kaveh Razavi, has produced TRRespass (in collaboration with VUSec), Blacksmith, Half-Double (with the Graz group), ProTRR, ZenHammer, McSee, and Phoenix. The group's signature contribution has been the combination of FPGA-based DRAM characterisation with commodity-platform exploitation, producing both the deepest mechanism-level reverse engineering and the most reliable end-to-end exploitation in the literature. The group's open-source release practice — Blacksmith, ZenHammer, Phoenix all have published source code with documentation — has set the reproducibility standard for the field.

**SAFARI at ETH Zurich and Carnegie Mellon**, led by Onur Mutlu, has produced the original Kim et al. ISCA 2014 paper, the SoftMC FPGA platform, the DRAM Bender extension, BlockHammer, the various U-TRR papers, RowPress, the wordline-voltage characterisation, and dozens of additional characterisation papers spanning DRAM, NAND flash, and emerging memory technologies. The group's signature contribution has been the characterisation-paper genre, with the template of large sample size, multi-vendor coverage, exhaustive parameter sweep, and numbered observations.

**VUSec at VU Amsterdam**, with Cristiano Giuffrida, Herbert Bos, and former PhD student Kaveh Razavi, has produced Drammer, Flip Feng Shui, TRRespass (with COMSEC), SMASH, and the DRAMA addressing-function methodology. The group's signature contribution has been the exploit-engineering side of the literature, producing the end-to-end exploits that motivate the mechanism-level work.

**Graz TU**, with Daniel Gruss and former PhD students including Andreas Kogler and Jonas Juffinger, has produced Rowhammer.js, the sudo binary exploit, Half-Double (with Google), and PressHammer. The group's signature contribution has been the JavaScript and software-side Rowhammer work plus the methodological rigor of the falsification-based experimental design (Half-Double's null-hypothesis falsification methodology).

**Google Chromium Security Team**, with Salman Qazi, Yoongu Kim, Nicolas Boichat, Eric Shiu, and Mattias Nissler, has produced the industry-side contributions to Half-Double and provided ongoing support for academic disclosure coordination. The group's signature contribution has been the integration of academic Rowhammer research with the Chromebook deployment context, providing both a target environment and a disclosure-coordination role.

**Academic mitigation groups**, including Moinuddin Qureshi at Georgia Tech (Randomized Row-Swap), Jung Ho Ahn at Seoul National University (Graphene), Michele Marazzi at ETH (ProTRR), and the QPRAC group at UBC and NVIDIA, have produced the principled-mitigation side of the literature. The mitigation groups overlap less with the attack groups than is typical in security research; the result is that mitigation proposals are developed in parallel with rather than in direct response to specific attacks.

## Appendix on the Cross-Tenant Threat Model

The cloud co-tenancy threat model has been a recurring motivator for the Rowhammer literature, beginning with Flip Feng Shui (Razavi et al., USENIX Security 2016) and continuing through the Cloud Rowhammer paper (Cojocar et al., S&P 2020) and the various subsequent papers that test on virtualised platforms.

The threat model assumes that an attacker controls a virtual machine on a cloud provider's infrastructure and that the victim is a co-tenant VM on the same physical host, sharing the same DRAM module. The attacker uses Rowhammer to induce bit flips in pages that are physically located in the same DRAM rows as the victim's data, with the bit flips corrupting victim memory in ways that the attacker can exploit. Specific exploitation pathways depend on the cloud provider's memory management: page deduplication (combining identical pages across VMs) creates the most direct cross-tenant attack surface; same-host scheduling without deduplication creates a weaker attack surface that requires more pre-attack reconnaissance.

The cloud-tenancy threat model has driven specific mitigation deployments. Several major cloud providers (Google, AWS, Microsoft Azure, Oracle Cloud) have, on the basis of public statements, disabled inter-tenant page deduplication in production. Some providers reserve specific DIMM modules for higher-tier customers (the Siloz proposal at SOSP 2023 formalises this with bank-level isolation). The deployed mitigations are not always disclosed in detail; the public information consists of statements that mitigations exist and inferential evidence from academic test campaigns that include cloud platforms.

The Cloud Rowhammer paper (Cojocar et al., S&P 2020) deserves separate mention because it provides the methodology for testing the cloud-tenancy threat model. The methodology includes techniques for identifying contiguous physical regions in a virtualised environment without `/proc/pagemap` access, techniques for triggering bit flips in the limited memory budget that a typical cloud VM provides, and techniques for verifying bit flip occurrence in the presence of ECC. The methodology has been adopted by subsequent cloud-focused Rowhammer evaluations and forms the practical basis for cloud-side defensive testing.

The cross-tenant threat model is one of the few areas where vendor and academic responses have converged operationally rather than only in standards work. The defensive measures deployed in production cloud environments are concrete; the absence of a public protocol for evaluating those measures means that the academic literature is the primary source of evidence about their effectiveness. The relationship between the two is more collaborative than the broader vendor-academic relationship in the Rowhammer field, possibly because cloud providers have a direct operational interest in mitigation effectiveness that DRAM vendors do not share.

## Appendix on the Software Exploit Layer

The exploit layer that sits on top of the Rowhammer bit-flip-triggering primitive is largely shared across the decade of literature. The same three classical exploit targets — Page Table Entries, RSA-2048 modulus bits, sudo binary opcodes — have been used by Seaborn and Dullien (2015), Razavi et al. (2016), Gruss et al. (2018), TRRespass (2020), Blacksmith (2022), ZenHammer (2024), and Phoenix (2026). The shared use reflects the fact that the exploit framework is decoupled from the bit-flip-triggering layer: improvements to the triggering layer translate directly to improvements in exploitation speed and reliability, without requiring new exploit constructions.

The PTE attack is the most general of the three and is the first that papers in this lineage demonstrate. The attack flips a bit in the Physical Frame Number field of a Page Table Entry, redirecting the virtual page that the PTE controls to a different physical frame. The redirected frame is one that the attacker controls (typically by having a file-backed mapping that the OS placed at the target frame). The attacker reads or writes the redirected frame and gains primitive R/W to the contents of the original PTE's intended target — which, if the original PTE held a page table, is another PTE in the kernel page-table hierarchy. The PTE attack typically yields a page-table R/W primitive that escalates to root in seconds.

The RSA-2048 attack targets long-lived public keys in memory. The attack flips a bit in the RSA modulus stored in the page cache (typically the modulus of an SSH authorised_keys public key). The flipped modulus is, with non-negligible probability (12 to 22 percent depending on which bit and module), factorable into primes smaller than the modulus. The factorisation allows the attacker to reconstruct the corresponding private key and authenticate as the legitimate user. The attack requires that the targeted public key be in memory at the time of the bit flip, that the attacker have read access to the page containing the key, and that the flipped modulus survive the OS-level write-back of the page cache.

The sudo binary attack targets the in-memory copy of the sudo binary. The attack flips one of a small number of opcode bits in the binary's authentication-check code path, converting a conditional branch into an unconditional one or otherwise bypassing the check. The attack requires that the targeted bit be one of approximately 29 known-vulnerable opcode bits within a 4 KiB page of the binary, which corresponds to an exploitability fraction of approximately 0.088% of the bits in the page. The sudo attack has the narrowest exploitability and is the hardest of the three.

The three exploits together establish that the exploitation surface is broad enough that any sufficiently-capable bit-flip-triggering primitive produces an exploit. The PTE attack is exploitable on essentially every module that produces any bit flips at all; the RSA-2048 attack is exploitable on most modules; the sudo attack is exploitable on the most-vulnerable modules. The progression provides a natural difficulty hierarchy that papers in this lineage use to characterise the practical impact of their bit-flip findings.

## Appendix on the FPGA Platforms

The FPGA-based characterisation platforms that the field has developed deserve brief mention because they are the primary instruments of the empirical work.

SoftMC (Hassan et al., HPCA 2017) is the foundational FPGA platform. It implements a programmable memory controller on a Xilinx FPGA, exposing every DRAM command (ACT, PRE, RD, WR, REF) at cycle-accurate granularity through a high-level Python or C++ interface. The platform supports DDR3 in its original release and was extended for DDR4 by the TRRespass authors in their methodology. SoftMC is the basis for nearly all in-DRAM TRR characterisation in the public literature; without it, the reverse-engineering work in TRRespass, U-TRR, and the related papers would not have been feasible.

DRAM Bender (Olgun et al., 2022) is the SAFARI group's extension of SoftMC for DDR4 characterisation work. It supports additional command-injection patterns, temperature control integration (via the MaxWell FT200 PID controller used in the RowPress paper), and longer-running characterisation campaigns. DRAM Bender is the platform used in the 164-chip RowPress characterisation and in the subsequent wordline-voltage and asymmetric-pattern work.

Antmicro LiteX Rowhammer Tester is an open-source FPGA platform developed by Antmicro and used in the Half-Double FPGA validation work. It is based on the LiteX SoC framework and supports DDR4 on a ZCU104 development board. The platform is less full-featured than SoftMC and DRAM Bender but is open-source from end to end (including the firmware and host-side software), which has supported its adoption by smaller research groups.

Antmicro DDR5 Tester is the DDR5 successor to the LiteX Rowhammer Tester, used by the Phoenix paper. The Phoenix paper documents four bugs in the platform that were identified and fixed during the project, representing approximately one person-year of FPGA debugging effort. The bug fixes are included in the public release of the Phoenix source. The four-bug debugging cost illustrates the integration challenges that DDR5 introduces compared to DDR4: tighter timing constraints, larger command vocabulary (RFM, PRAC Alert), more complex per-bank logic.

The FPGA platforms collectively constitute the experimental substrate of the field. Without them, the empirical work that has falsified vendor claims about TRR would not have been possible; with them, the work has been reproducible across research groups. The open-source nature of the platforms is part of the methodological hygiene of the field; the alternative — proprietary characterisation tools with restricted distribution — would have changed the field's epistemic structure significantly.

## Appendix on Terminology

The Rowhammer literature has accumulated a substantial technical vocabulary across the decade. The terms below are consolidated from the various papers and standards for reference.

**ACT** — Activate command. Issued by the memory controller to raise the wordline of a specified row in a specified bank. Initiates row activation; required before any read or write to a row.

**Activation count** — Number of times a row has been activated within a refresh window. Reset to zero by the next REFRESH that targets the row.

**Aggressor row** — A row whose activation contributes disturbance to a victim row. In a single-sided pattern, one aggressor at distance 1. In a double-sided pattern, two aggressors at distance 1 on either side of the victim. In a many-sided pattern, three or more aggressors.

**ABO (Alert Back-Off) protocol** — The protocol that PRAC uses to signal threshold crossing to the memory controller. Comprises NORMAL, PRE_RECOVERY, RECOVERY, and DELAY states.

**ABOACT** — The number of activations the memory controller may issue between the assertion of the Alert pin and the issuance of the corresponding RFM commands. Specified as 3 activations (approximately 180 nanoseconds) in the JEDEC PRAC specification.

**ABODelay** — The minimum number of activations between successive Alert assertions, after the RECOVERY state completes.

**Alert pin** — A dedicated pin on the DDR5 interface used by the DRAM to signal a PRAC threshold crossing to the memory controller.

**Blast radius** — The set of rows around an activated row that receive non-negligible disturbance. Distance-1 has the largest contribution; distance-2 contributes approximately one-tenth; greater distances contribute progressively less.

**CLFLUSH / CLFLUSHOPT** — x86 cache-line flush instructions used in Rowhammer attacks to ensure that subsequent loads bypass the cache and reach DRAM.

**DRAMA** — DRAM addressing reverse-engineering methodology (Pessl et al., USENIX Security 2016).

**ECC** — Error Correcting Code. On-die ECC in DDR5 provides single-error-correction, double-error-detection per 128-bit codeword.

**Half-Double pattern** — The (F+ → F−)^∞ Quad pattern that activates far aggressors at distance 2, with distance-1 activations contributed by the TRR inhibitor.

**HCmin** — Minimum activation count required to trigger a bit flip on the most vulnerable cell of a module, under specified pattern and environmental conditions.

**MAC (Maximum Activate Count)** — JEDEC SPD field specifying the maximum activations a row can sustain. Values 0b0001-0b0110 specify thresholds 200,000 to 700,000; 0b1000 specifies "unlimited"; 0b0000 specifies "untested."

**Memory Hoisting** — AMD-specific physical-address-space remapping that perturbs DRAM addressing-function recovery on Zen platforms.

**NBO** — The PRAC activation threshold at which the Alert is asserted. Configured below the Rowhammer threshold TRH.

**Nmit** — The number of RFM commands the memory controller issues per ABO cycle. Specified as 1, 2, or 4 in the JEDEC specification.

**ODECC** — On-die ECC, standardised in DDR5.

**PRAC** — Per Row Activation Counting. JEDEC JESD79-5C mitigation that places an activation counter on each DRAM row.

**pTRR** — Pseudo-TRR. Intel memory-controller-side mitigation in Ivy Bridge EP server CPUs, reading the SPD MAC field.

**PSQ (Priority-Based Service Queue)** — QPRAC's per-bank queue, sorted by activation count, that determines which rows receive RFM-driven mitigation.

**RFM** — Refresh Management. JEDEC DDR5 command issued by the memory controller to trigger DRAM-side mitigation.

**RFMab / RFMsb / RFMpb** — Variants of RFM: all-bank, same-bank, per-bank. The JEDEC specification supports RFMab; same-bank and per-bank variants are proposed in academic literature.

**SoftMC** — FPGA-based memory controller (Hassan et al., HPCA 2017) used for cycle-accurate DRAM characterisation.

**SPD** — Serial Presence Detect. ROM on a DIMM containing module identification and configuration data, including the MAC field.

**tAggON** — Row-open time. Duration that a row is held in the open state (between ACT and PRE).

**tRAS** — Minimum row-active time. JEDEC parameter; approximately 32-35 ns for DDR4.

**tRC** — Minimum row-cycle time. JEDEC parameter; approximately 45-50 ns for DDR4.

**tRCD** — Activate-to-read delay. JEDEC parameter; approximately 13 ns for DDR4-3200.

**tREFI** — Average refresh interval. JEDEC parameter; 7.8 µs for DDR4, 3.9 µs for DDR5.

**tREFW** — Refresh window. Total time within which every row must be refreshed; 64 ms for DDR4, 32 ms for DDR5.

**tRFC** — Refresh cycle time. The interval during which a REFRESH command occupies a bank.

**tRP** — Precharge time. JEDEC parameter; approximately 13 ns for DDR4-3200.

**TRH** — Rowhammer Threshold. Activation count at which a bit flip becomes possible on a vulnerable cell.

**TRR (Target Row Refresh)** — In-DRAM mitigation that refreshes the neighbours of recently activated rows. Vendor-specific implementation, undocumented in DDR4 datasheets.

**TRRespass** — Many-sided pattern class that overflows the TRR sampler (Frigo et al., S&P 2020).

**Victim row** — A row that is the target of disturbance; the row whose cells the attacker hopes to flip.

**Wordline** — The control line that raises the gate of every cell in a row when activated. The wordline voltage transition is the proximate cause of read disturbance on adjacent rows.

## Appendix on the Reproducibility Movement

The reproducibility of Rowhammer papers in the academic record has been driven by two converging trends. The first is the broader open-artifact movement in computer science conferences, which has, since approximately 2015, encouraged or required authors to release executable artifacts alongside their papers. The Rowhammer literature has participated in this movement from an early date: TRRespass was an early adopter of full source release; Blacksmith continued the pattern; ZenHammer and Phoenix have been released as fully reproducible artifacts including the FPGA bug fixes documented in their respective appendices.

The second is the structural necessity of reproducibility in DRAM research. The empirical claims of Rowhammer papers are typically claims about specific DIMM modules tested under specific conditions; without the ability to reproduce the experiments on the same or comparable modules, the claims cannot be evaluated by other researchers. The reproducibility requirement is therefore stronger than in software-security research, where the underlying system is more uniformly accessible. The published artifacts include not only source code but also DIMM identification information, FPGA platform versions, environmental conditions (temperature, voltage), and characterisation data.

Independent reproductions of major Rowhammer papers have generally confirmed the original findings. The BlacksmithRepro work by the Graz group at ESORICS 2023 reproduced the Blacksmith bit-flip generation on 10 of the original DIMM models, noting that ambient temperature and BIOS settings affected the bit-flip count more than the source paper had anticipated but that the qualitative findings held. Similar reproductions of TRRespass on the original test set have been performed by multiple groups. The ZenHammer reproductions have been less common because the AMD Zen test platforms are less widely available than the Intel platforms used in earlier work.

The reproducibility record establishes that the Rowhammer field is in the empirical tier of computer security research, where claims can be evaluated by independent replication. The vendor side of the field is not yet at this tier: vendor claims about mitigation effectiveness, deployment scope, and product roadmap cannot be independently verified, and the empirical work that exists is conducted by academic researchers without vendor cooperation. The asymmetry between the academic and vendor sides is a structural feature of the field, with implications for how readers should evaluate claims from each source.

## Appendix on the Long-Term Persistence of Vulnerable Hardware

A consequence of the disclosure-to-deployment latency for hardware mitigations is that the hardware deployed at any given time is, in general, vulnerable to attacks that will be disclosed later. The implication is that the deployed hardware base in 2026 — DDR3 in legacy systems, DDR4 in the majority of consumer and enterprise systems, DDR5 in the most recent systems — is collectively vulnerable to the full historical set of Rowhammer attacks, with the substrate vulnerability of older parts compounded by the inferior mitigations they were shipped with.

The deployed base of DDR3 modules manufactured 2010-2014 will be in production use for the lifetime of the systems they ship in; some embedded and industrial systems with long replacement cycles will continue to use DDR3 into the 2030s. The DDR3 substrate is, per the original Kim et al. measurements, vulnerable at HCmin of 139,000 activations, which is reachable within seconds of execution; the mitigations available for DDR3 (refresh doubling, Intel pTRR on a small subset of CPUs) are bypassable by the original 2014 methodology and have not been improved.

The deployed base of DDR4 modules manufactured 2016-2024 is the majority of the current commodity-system installed base. Each module in this base has the in-DRAM TRR mitigation of its manufacturing date; modules manufactured before approximately 2020 have the first-generation TRR that TRRespass bypassed in 2020; modules manufactured 2020-2024 have second-generation TRR that Blacksmith bypassed in 2022. No deployed DDR4 module has a mitigation that has not been bypassed in the public literature; the question for any specific deployment is which bypass methodology applies, not whether one applies.

The deployed base of DDR5 modules manufactured 2021-2026 is the recent and growing addition. SK Hynix modules in this range have the TRR mechanisms that Phoenix characterised in 2026; Samsung and Micron modules in this range have not been characterised in the public literature but are likely to have similar mechanisms. Modules manufactured after the JEDEC PRAC specification ratification in July 2024 may or may not have PRAC support, depending on vendor product roadmaps; the public information on which products have PRAC is incomplete.

The implication is that the Rowhammer threat will persist in the deployed hardware base for the lifetime of the deployed systems, with each generation's threat dependent on which mitigation the generation shipped with and which attacks have since been published against that mitigation. The temporal gap between attack publication and hardware retirement is the persistence window; for typical commodity hardware lifecycles of 5-10 years, this window means that any attack published in 2020 will continue to apply to deployed hardware until approximately 2030. For embedded and industrial hardware with 20-year lifecycles, the window extends correspondingly. The Rowhammer threat is not, on this analysis, a problem that future hardware will solve; it is a problem that future hardware will solve for new deployments while leaving the existing base unmodified.

## Appendix on Future Directions

The active research questions in the Rowhammer literature at the time of writing fall into three categories. The first is the systematic exploration of the multi-axis attack space: combining the spatial, temporal, duration, and voltage axes that have been characterised separately to produce attacks that operate on combined axes simultaneously. The PressHammer work has demonstrated one such combination (distance + duration); future work is expected to combine more axes. The second is the deployment and evaluation of PRAC-class mitigations on production silicon: the JEDEC PRAC specification has been ratified, but commodity DDR5 silicon with deployed PRAC has only recently begun shipping. The third is the development of next-generation mitigations that address the residual weaknesses of PRAC (the ABOACT window, the queue-management latitude) and the cross-axis weaknesses (PRAC's blindness to row-open duration attacks).

The literature is likely to continue producing new attack papers at approximately the rate it has been producing them (roughly one per year for major attacks, plus several characterisation papers per year). The slope of substrate vulnerability across generations suggests that each generation will be more vulnerable than the previous one, requiring proportionally more capable mitigations. The slope of mitigation cost across generations suggests that the silicon-area and bandwidth overheads will continue to grow, with the question of whether the cost remains acceptable to vendors at each generation being a recurring open question.

The longer-term trajectory of the Rowhammer story is uncertain. One possibility is that PRAC-class mitigations, combined with row-open duration tracking and other multi-axis defences, will produce a DRAM substrate that is genuinely Rowhammer-resistant at acceptable cost. A second possibility is that the trajectory of substrate vulnerability outpaces the development of mitigations, requiring more invasive architectural changes — DRAM redesign to eliminate the substrate vulnerability, memory hierarchy redesign to isolate vulnerable subsystems, or system-level isolation that does not depend on substrate properties. A third possibility is that the trajectory continues indefinitely, with each generation of substrate producing new mitigation work and each mitigation generation producing new attack work. The current evidence does not allow a determination among the three possibilities.

## Appendix on the Cost of Reverse Engineering

The Phoenix paper is unusually candid about the cost of the work it documents. Section 8.1 of the Phoenix paper estimates the project cost at approximately two person-years total, distributed across six authors and approximately four calendar years of effort. The breakdown includes approximately one person-year for FPGA platform debugging (the four bugs in the Antmicro DDR5 Tester that the paper documents in Appendix A), approximately half a person-year for the H2 and H6 TRR reverse engineering, and approximately half a person-year for the self-correcting synchronisation development. The transparency is uncommon in academic papers; the typical paper does not document its own project cost.

The estimate is informative for understanding the replication economics of the field. Two person-years is a substantial investment, particularly when distributed across a four-year calendar window in which the participants have other commitments. The investment is feasible for established research groups with FPGA infrastructure and graduate-student bandwidth; it is not feasible for newcomer groups without the supporting infrastructure. The implication is that the empirical base of the field is concentrated in a small number of established groups, with the replication costs creating a structural barrier to entry.

The Phoenix paper's estimate for extending the methodology to additional DRAM vendors (Samsung, Micron) is approximately half a person-year per vendor for the TRR characterisation alone, plus the platform extension to support the vendor's specific timing parameters. Extending to additional CPU vendors (Intel) adds further synchronisation tuning. The cumulative cost of comprehensive vendor and platform coverage is on the order of one to two additional person-years per (vendor, CPU) pair. The literature's coverage is therefore necessarily partial; any given paper addresses a specific subset of the (vendor, CPU, generation) space, and broader coverage requires multiple papers across multiple research groups.

The grey-box methodology that the Phoenix paper describes — combining limited reverse engineering with black-box fuzzing — is in part a response to the cost structure. By identifying the structural features of a TRR mechanism (sampling period, lightly-sampled intervals, sampled ACT-slot positions) via targeted reverse engineering and then using these features to guide black-box pattern generation, the methodology reduces the per-pattern fuzzing cost while preserving the exhaustiveness of black-box search. The total cost of the grey-box approach is intermediate between pure reverse engineering (Phoenix's approach) and pure black-box fuzzing (Blacksmith's approach); the field has converged on grey-box approaches as the most cost-effective for new substrates.

## Appendix on the Disclosure-Coordination Bodies

Three national CERT bodies have appeared in the Rowhammer disclosure literature, with substantively different roles. The Dutch National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC-NL) coordinated the TRRespass disclosure in November 2019, the Drammer disclosure in 2016, and several intermediate disclosures involving VUSec-affiliated authors. The Dutch role reflects VU Amsterdam's location and the historical relationship between the VUSec group and the Dutch national infrastructure. The Swiss National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC-CH) coordinated the Blacksmith disclosure in 2021 and the Phoenix disclosure in 2025-2026. The Swiss role reflects ETH Zurich's location and the COMSEC group's coordination with the Swiss national infrastructure. The German BSI (Bundesamt für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik) has appeared in disclosures involving Graz TU and TU Darmstadt authors, though less prominently than the Dutch and Swiss bodies.

The pattern of national-CERT involvement reflects the geographic distribution of the major Rowhammer research groups. The academic side of the literature has been concentrated in Europe and the United States; the corresponding national-CERT bodies handle disclosure coordination for the research groups in their jurisdictions. The vendor side is concentrated in East Asia (Samsung in Korea, SK Hynix in Korea, Micron in the United States) and the United States; the geographic mismatch between the academic and vendor sides has not, in the public literature, produced significant friction, though the embargo durations (nine months for Blacksmith, fifteen months for Half-Double) suggest that cross-jurisdictional coordination is a non-trivial element of the process.

Industry vulnerability databases — CVE, CWE, NVD — have assigned identifiers to several Rowhammer attacks: CVE-2020-10255 for TRRespass, CVE-2021-42114 for Blacksmith, CVE-2025-6202 for Phoenix. Half-Double did not receive a dedicated CVE; the disclosure treated it as a class of attacks rather than a specific vulnerability. The CVE assignments serve primarily as bibliographic anchors rather than as references to specific patchable software; the underlying vulnerabilities are in DRAM hardware that is not patchable in the conventional sense.

## Appendix B — A Note on the Methodology of This Writeup

The writeup synthesises eight source documents from the author's vault on the Rowhammer literature. Each source document is itself a synthesis of one or more primary academic papers and ancillary materials (CVE records, vendor disclosures, source-code repositories). The synthesis preserves the factual content of the source documents while restructuring it into the anatomical register described in the Author's Note.

Two methodological choices in the synthesis bear separate explanation. The first is the choice to organise the material by physical and architectural locus rather than by chronological order alone. The Rowhammer literature is naturally chronological — each paper responds to the assumptions of its predecessors — but the chronological structure obscures the architectural relationships among the papers. TRRespass and Blacksmith are both DDR4 TRR-bypass papers; Half-Double is a distance-axis paper; RowPress is a duration-axis paper; Phoenix is a DDR5 TRR-bypass paper that builds on TRRespass's methodology. Organising by architectural locus allows the reader to see which papers occupy which slots in a more general framework, without losing the temporal information that the citation table preserves.

The second is the choice to close each section with an "Assumption invalidated" paragraph. The format is borrowed from the falsificationist tradition in philosophy of science (Popper, "The Logic of Scientific Discovery") and applied to the empirical security literature. The Rowhammer field is unusual in that it has been driven, more than most security subfields, by negative results: TRRespass falsified vendor claims of immunity, Blacksmith falsified the uniformity assumption, RowPress falsified the single-axis assumption, Phoenix falsified the DDR5-immunity assumption. Reading the literature as a sequence of falsifications honours the field's actual epistemic structure better than reading it as a sequence of new attacks. The closing paragraphs are not rhetorical embellishment; they are the structural backbone of the writeup.

## Appendix on the Synchronisation Problem in Detail

The synchronisation problem in modern Rowhammer attacks deserves a separate appendix because it has become one of the dominant engineering challenges of the field. The basic problem is that an attacker's hammering pattern must align with the REFRESH commands issued by the memory controller in order to hit the TRR sampler's blind spots. The pattern must execute within the refresh intervals between REFRESHes, and the boundaries of those intervals must be known to the attacker.

In the simplest case (DDR3 without TRR), there is no synchronisation requirement: the attacker hammers continuously and refreshes occur in the background without affecting the attack. In DDR4 with TRR, the attacker must align with REFRESH boundaries because the TRR mitigation acts during REFRESH commands; misalignment causes the TRR to refresh the wrong victim rows. In DDR5 with longer TRR sampling periods, the alignment requirement becomes more stringent because the misalignment cost compounds across thousands of refresh intervals.

The synchronisation methodology has evolved across the literature. The original TRRespass methodology synchronised by measuring REF-to-REF intervals via timing side channels and aligning pattern starts with detected REFRESH events. The Blacksmith methodology refined this with a `ref_sync` routine that loops on `rdtscp` measurements to detect the latency spike that REFRESH induces. The ZenHammer methodology extended this to a continuous-non-repeating measurement that eliminates the untimed gap between iterations. The Phoenix methodology accepts measurement loss and adds self-correction: missed REFRESHes are detected after the fact via timestamp delta thresholds, and the pattern execution is advanced to recover.

The progression of methodologies reflects a progression of attack environments. DDR3 attacks had effectively no synchronisation requirement. DDR4 attacks on Intel had a moderate synchronisation requirement that the Blacksmith methodology satisfied. DDR4 attacks on AMD Zen had a harder requirement that the Blacksmith methodology silently failed on, requiring the ZenHammer continuous-measurement fix. DDR5 attacks on Zen 4 had a still-harder requirement that the ZenHammer methodology was insufficient for, requiring the Phoenix self-correcting fix. Each generation has produced a synchronisation challenge that the previous generation's methodology cannot meet.

The implication for future generations is that the synchronisation methodology will continue to evolve. DDR6, with its smaller tREFI and projected longer TRR sampling periods, will likely require a still more capable synchronisation framework. The Phoenix self-correcting methodology may generalise to DDR6 with parameter tuning, or it may require structural extension. The pattern suggests that synchronisation will remain one of the dominant engineering challenges of Rowhammer attacks for the foreseeable future, with each new substrate requiring synchronisation work as a research contribution in its own right.

## Appendix on Composition with Other Microarchitectural Attacks

The composition of Rowhammer with other microarchitectural attacks is one of the more interesting cross-cutting themes of the post-2020 literature. The Half-Double Spectre oracle is the clearest case: Spectre, normally a confidentiality attack producing read primitives over architecturally inaccessible memory, is used as a crash-safe oracle that determines whether a candidate PTE address is valid. The use is structurally interesting because it converts a defensive property of ARM Cortex-A cores (exception suppression preventing Meltdown-style transient leaks) into an offensive primitive that supports the blind-hammering exploitation.

The SpecHammer paper (2022) generalises the Half-Double composition to Intel platforms, where the exception-suppression properties are different but a similar composition is achievable with adjusted gadget construction. The composition pattern is broadly applicable: any microarchitectural attack that produces a binary oracle (cache hit / cache miss, transient store-buffer state, branch-predictor update) can be used as a verification oracle for a memory-corruption primitive that lacks direct read-back capability.

Memory encryption (AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX) does not prevent Rowhammer in the basic sense: the bit-flip mechanism operates below the encryption layer, and a flipped bit in encrypted memory corrupts the encrypted ciphertext, which decrypts to corrupted plaintext. The integrity protection in SEV-SNP and TDX may detect the corruption (treating it as an integrity violation and refusing to use the corrupted data), but does not prevent the underlying flip. Whether the integrity-violation response is exploitable as a side channel is an open research question; the response is typically a fault that terminates the VM, but the timing and resource-consumption patterns of the fault may leak information.

The composition with cache side channels has been explored in the Flush+Reload and Flush+Flush literature; the explicit Rowhammer + cache-side-channel compositions have not been systematically surveyed but appear in pieces across multiple papers. The trend suggests that the microarchitectural attack surface is multiplicative rather than additive: each primitive composed with each other primitive produces a distinct exploitation pathway, and the total number of pathways grows combinatorially with the number of primitives. Defending against the surface therefore requires defences that apply to each primitive independently; defending against a specific composition is generally insufficient because the same primitives can be composed differently.

## Appendix on Concluding Observations

The Rowhammer literature is, in 2026, a mature subfield of hardware security with a recognisable structure: a substrate-level phenomenon characterised across multiple generations, a multi-axis attack space mapped through a decade of publications, a mitigation arc that has converged on counter-based defences with formal security bounds, and a research-group ecosystem distributed across Europe and North America that produces a steady output of attack and mitigation papers.

The writeup has read the literature anatomically: as a description of a single physical phenomenon that adapts to every architectural and procedural counter-design that vendors have shipped against it. The reading is not the only possible one. An attack-oriented reading would emphasise the technique innovations of each paper as practical capabilities. A defence-oriented reading would emphasise the mitigation gaps that each attack exposes as priorities for the next generation. A vendor-oriented reading would emphasise the production-economics constraints that have shaped the deployed mitigation set. Each reading captures part of the field; none captures the whole.

The anatomical reading has one advantage that the alternatives lack: it preserves the relationship between the empirical findings and the assumptions they invalidate. Reading the literature as a sequence of falsifications, rather than as a sequence of capability disclosures, places the substrate-level reality and the assumption-level claims in direct relation. The substrate behaves as it has behaved since 2014; the assumptions about how it would behave have, in sequence, been falsified by careful measurement. The remaining assumptions — about PRAC, about future generations, about composition with other phenomena — are hypotheses that subsequent measurement will test.

The piece does not predict what the next decade will produce. The trajectory of substrate vulnerability suggests that DDR6 and beyond will require mitigations more capable than the current PRAC framework. The trajectory of attack methodology suggests that the synchronisation, distance, and duration axes will continue to be explored, with new axes likely to emerge as characterisation techniques improve. The trajectory of mitigation deployment suggests that the disclosure-to-deployment latency will continue to leave deployed hardware vulnerable for the duration of its lifecycle. Whether these trajectories continue, accelerate, or reverse will depend on choices that the JEDEC standards process, the major DRAM vendors, and the academic research community make over the coming years.

The writeup ends here because the literature ends here, in 2026, with Phoenix as the most recent attack and QPRAC as the most recent published mitigation proposal. The next paper is presumably being written. The next assumption is presumably being tested. The piece may be updated when both are published.
